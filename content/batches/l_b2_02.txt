=== b2-the-bilingual-brain | The Advantages of a Bilingual Brain | Education | B2
More than half of the world's population speaks at least two languages. For many people, switching between languages is a natural part of daily life, whether they speak one language at home and another at work, or use several languages with friends from different backgrounds. In recent decades, researchers have become increasingly interested in how bilingualism affects the brain.

For much of the twentieth century, many educators believed that raising children with two languages could cause confusion and slow down their development. Some parents who had immigrated to a new country were even advised to stop speaking their native language to their children. Today, this view has largely been abandoned. Research has shown that bilingual children reach the same language milestones as monolingual children, although they may know slightly fewer words in each language at first.

One of the most interesting areas of research concerns what are known as executive functions. These are mental skills that help us plan, focus our attention, and switch between tasks. When bilingual people speak, both of their languages appear to be active in the brain at the same time. To speak in one language, they must constantly suppress the other. Some researchers believe that this constant practice strengthens the brain's control systems.

Several studies have found that bilingual people perform better on certain tasks that require switching attention or ignoring distractions. There is also some evidence that bilingualism may help to protect the brain in old age. Studies of older adults have suggested that bilingual people may develop symptoms of dementia several years later than monolingual people, even though they do not avoid the disease altogether.

However, these findings are not universally accepted. In recent years, some researchers have failed to reproduce the bilingual advantage in carefully controlled experiments. They argue that other factors, such as education, income, and cultural background, may explain some of the differences observed in earlier studies.

Even if the cognitive benefits turn out to be smaller than once thought, few people doubt the social and cultural advantages of speaking more than one language. Bilingualism allows people to communicate with a wider range of people, to access different cultures and ways of thinking, and to maintain a connection with their family heritage.

=== b2-the-detective-in-the-lab | Solving Crimes with Science | Science | B2
In popular television dramas, forensic scientists solve crimes in a matter of minutes. A single hair or a drop of blood is analyzed with impressive machines, and the identity of the criminal appears on a screen just before the commercial break. Real forensic science is rarely so fast or so certain, but it has nonetheless transformed the way crimes are investigated.

One of the oldest forensic techniques is fingerprint analysis. In the late nineteenth century, scientists discovered that the patterns of ridges on our fingertips are unique to each individual and remain the same throughout life. Fingerprints were soon being used by police forces around the world. Today, computer databases can compare a fingerprint found at a crime scene with millions of records in seconds.

The most important breakthrough in modern forensics, however, came in the nineteen eighties with the development of DNA profiling. Every person, except identical twins, has a unique DNA code. By analyzing DNA from blood, saliva, skin cells, or hair left at a crime scene, scientists can link a suspect to a crime with a high degree of probability. DNA evidence has helped to convict criminals in cases that had remained unsolved for decades.

Equally significantly, DNA evidence has been used to prove the innocence of people who were wrongly convicted. In the United States alone, hundreds of prisoners have been released after DNA tests showed they could not have committed the crimes for which they were imprisoned. Some had spent more than twenty years in prison.

Forensic science is not infallible, though. Evidence can be contaminated, samples can be mixed up, and results can be misinterpreted. Some techniques that were once considered reliable, such as comparing the marks left by teeth, have been criticized for lacking a solid scientific basis. Experts warn that juries may place too much trust in scientific evidence, partly because of the way it is presented in television shows.

New technologies continue to emerge. Investigators can now analyze tiny amounts of DNA and even use family history databases to identify unknown suspects through their relatives. These advances raise important questions about privacy and the balance between solving crimes and protecting individual rights.

=== b2-the-forgotten-war-dog | The Pigeon Who Saved Two Hundred Soldiers | History | B2
In October nineteen eighteen, during the final months of the First World War, a group of more than five hundred American soldiers found themselves trapped behind enemy lines in the Argonne Forest in France. Surrounded by German troops, short of food and water, and running out of ammunition, they were in a desperate situation. Their commander, Major Charles Whittlesey, had only one way to communicate with headquarters: carrier pigeons.

Carrier pigeons were widely used in the war because they could find their way home over long distances, even through smoke and gunfire. Radio technology was still unreliable, and telephone lines were easily cut. Soldiers would write messages on small pieces of paper, place them in tiny containers attached to the birds' legs, and release them.

On the second day, the situation became even worse. American artillery, unaware of the soldiers' exact position, began firing shells that landed directly on them. Whittlesey sent pigeon after pigeon with messages explaining where they were, but the birds were shot down. Only one pigeon remained, a bird named Cher Ami, which means "dear friend" in French.

Whittlesey wrote a short, urgent message: "We are along the road parallel to two seven six point four. Our own artillery is dropping a barrage directly on us. For heaven's sake, stop it." The message was attached to Cher Ami's leg, and the bird was released.

As Cher Ami flew up, German soldiers opened fire. The pigeon was hit and fell, but then managed to rise again and continued flying. Around twenty-five minutes later, it arrived at its base, about forty kilometers away. It had been shot through the chest, had lost an eye, and its leg, still carrying the message, was hanging by only a few tendons. The artillery fire was stopped, and the surviving soldiers, about two hundred in total, were eventually rescued.

Army doctors worked hard to save Cher Ami's life, and even carved a small wooden leg for the bird. Cher Ami was awarded a French military medal for heroic service and became a celebrity. After the bird died the following year, its body was preserved, and it can still be seen today at a museum in Washington.

=== b2-the-quiet-revolution-in-farming | Farming Without Plowing | Environment | B2
For thousands of years, farmers have prepared their fields in the same basic way: by turning over the soil before planting seeds. Plowing buries weeds, loosens the earth, and mixes in fertilizers. It has long been seen as essential to good farming. But a growing number of farmers around the world are now abandoning the plow altogether, in a practice known as no-till farming.

The problem with traditional plowing is that it can damage the soil. Turning over the earth exposes it to wind and rain, which can wash or blow away the fertile top layer. This erosion is a serious problem in many parts of the world. In the nineteen thirties, a combination of intensive plowing and severe drought in the American Midwest led to the Dust Bowl, when huge clouds of soil were blown across the country and thousands of farming families were forced to leave their land.

Plowing also disrupts the complex ecosystem that lives in healthy soil, including earthworms, fungi, and countless microorganisms. These creatures help to create the structure of the soil and recycle nutrients. In addition, plowing releases carbon stored in the soil into the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, contributing to climate change.

In no-till farming, seeds are planted directly into the remains of the previous crop using special machines that cut narrow slots in the ground. The stems and leaves left on the surface act as a protective layer, reducing erosion and helping the soil to retain moisture. Many no-till farmers also plant "cover crops," such as clover or rye, between their main crops to keep the soil covered all year round.

Supporters say that no-till farming improves soil health, saves fuel and labor, and can help farms become more resilient to droughts. Over time, some farmers have reported higher yields and lower costs.

However, the method has its challenges. Without plowing to control weeds, many no-till farmers rely heavily on herbicides, which raises environmental concerns of its own. The transition can also be difficult, and yields may fall during the first few years while the soil adjusts. Nevertheless, the practice is spreading, and many experts believe that caring for soil will be one of the keys to feeding the world in a changing climate.

=== b2-the-imposter-syndrome | Feeling Like a Fraud | Psychology | B2
Imagine being promoted at work, winning an award, or getting into a top university, and instead of feeling proud, being convinced that it was all a mistake. You worry that you are not really as talented as people think, that you succeeded only because of luck, and that sooner or later, everybody will discover the truth. If this sounds familiar, you may have experienced what psychologists call imposter syndrome.

The term was first used in the nineteen seventies by two psychologists, Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes, who noticed that many highly successful women they studied believed they were not intelligent and had fooled everyone around them. Since then, research has shown that the feeling is very common among both men and women. Some studies suggest that around seventy percent of people experience it at some point in their lives.

People with imposter feelings often attribute their successes to external factors, such as luck, timing, or the help of others, while blaming their failures on their own lack of ability. As a result, success does little to increase their confidence. Many respond by working extremely hard to avoid being "found out," which can lead to stress, anxiety, and burnout. Others avoid new challenges altogether, fearing that they will not be able to cope.

Interestingly, imposter syndrome is particularly common among high achievers. Even famous people, including the writer Maya Angelou and the physicist Albert Einstein, are reported to have described feeling like frauds. It is also often experienced by people who are different from most of their colleagues, for example the first person in their family to go to university, or one of the few women in a male-dominated field.

There is no simple cure, but several strategies can help. The first is to recognize the feeling and to understand that it is common. Talking openly with friends, colleagues, or mentors often reveals that many others feel the same way. Keeping a record of achievements and positive feedback can help people see their successes more objectively.

It can also be helpful to change the way we think about competence. Nobody knows everything, and making mistakes is a normal part of learning. Accepting that we can be capable without being perfect may be the first step toward feeling that we truly deserve our success.

=== b2-the-memory-palace | The Art of Remembering | Psychology | B2
Every year, competitors at memory championships perform feats that seem almost impossible. Some memorize the order of a shuffled deck of fifty-two playing cards in less than twenty seconds. Others can recall hundreds of random numbers or the names and faces of dozens of strangers after studying them for just a few minutes. Remarkably, most of these memory champions insist that they were born with perfectly ordinary memories. Their secret is a set of techniques that are thousands of years old.

The most famous of these is the method of loci, also known as the memory palace. According to legend, it was invented by the ancient Greek poet Simonides. He was attending a banquet when he was called outside. While he was away, the roof of the hall collapsed, killing everyone inside. The bodies were so badly damaged that they could not be identified. But Simonides found he could remember who had been sitting where, by picturing the room in his mind. This experience made him realize that memory is closely connected to places.

The technique works like this. First, you imagine a place that you know well, such as your home or your route to work. Then, you mentally place the things you want to remember at specific locations along the way. To recall the information later, you simply take a mental walk through the place and see what you left there.

The key is to make the images as vivid, strange, and memorable as possible. If you need to remember to buy milk, eggs, and bread, you might imagine a river of milk flowing through your front door, a giant egg sitting on your sofa, and loaves of bread falling from the kitchen ceiling. The more unusual the image, the easier it is to recall.

Scientists believe the technique works because our brains evolved to be extremely good at remembering spaces and visual images. For our ancestors, remembering where to find food and water was essential for survival. Remembering lists of abstract information, however, was not.

Brain-scan studies have shown that memory champions use areas of the brain associated with spatial memory when they memorize. In one experiment, ordinary people who trained with the method of loci for six weeks significantly improved their memory, and the improvement lasted for months. It seems that with the right techniques, almost anyone can become a better rememberer.

=== b2-the-man-on-the-wire | Walking Between the Towers | Culture | B2
On the morning of the seventh of August, nineteen seventy-four, people on their way to work in Lower Manhattan began to stop and stare upward. Four hundred meters above the street, a tiny figure was walking on a wire stretched between the roofs of the two towers of the World Trade Center, which had only recently been completed. The man on the wire was a twenty-four-year-old French street performer named Philippe Petit.

Petit had dreamed of this walk for years, ever since he saw a picture of the towers in a magazine at a dentist's office, before they were even built. Preparing for it took him more than six years. He practiced on wires in fields in France, studied the buildings carefully, and made several secret visits to the towers, sometimes disguised as a construction worker or a journalist.

The walk itself was completely illegal. On the night before, Petit and his team of friends hid in the towers with their equipment. During the night, they used a bow and arrow to shoot a fishing line from one tower to the other. Using this line, they pulled across ropes and then the heavy steel cable that Petit would walk on. The work took hours, and several times the team came close to being discovered by security guards.

Shortly after seven o'clock in the morning, Petit stepped onto the wire. For about forty-five minutes, he crossed back and forth between the towers eight times. He walked, knelt, lay down on the wire, and even saluted the crowds below. Police officers waited on the roofs, but they could do nothing until he decided to come back.

When he finally stepped off the wire, he was arrested. However, the public reaction was so enthusiastic that the charges were soon dropped, on the condition that he give a free performance for children in Central Park. The walk made him world famous.

Decades later, after the towers were destroyed in the attacks of two thousand one, Petit's walk took on a new meaning. A documentary about the event won an Academy Award, and many people saw his daring performance as a celebration of beauty, courage, and the human spirit in a place now associated with tragedy.

=== b2-is-social-media-making-us-unhappy | Social Media and Mental Health | Technology | B2
Over the past fifteen years, social media has become an integral part of daily life for billions of people, particularly young people. Many teenagers spend several hours a day on platforms where they share photos, watch videos, and follow the lives of friends and celebrities. During the same period, rates of anxiety and depression among adolescents have risen significantly in several countries. Many people have concluded that social media is to blame. But is the evidence really that clear?

Some researchers argue strongly that social media is harming young people's mental health. They point out that the rise in mental health problems began around the early two thousand tens, roughly when smartphones became widespread. They suggest that social media encourages constant comparison with others, who often present idealized versions of their lives. Seeing carefully edited images of attractive, successful, and happy people may make users feel inadequate. Social media can also expose young people to cyberbullying and harmful content, and time spent online may replace sleep, exercise, and face-to-face social contact.

Other researchers urge caution. They note that many studies on the topic show only a correlation between social media use and poor mental health, which does not prove that one causes the other. It is possible that young people who are already unhappy spend more time online, rather than the other way around. Some large studies have found that the overall link between screen time and well-being is relatively small. Furthermore, social media is not the same for everyone. For some young people, particularly those who feel isolated, online communities can provide valuable support and a sense of belonging.

What most experts agree on is that how people use social media may matter more than how much they use it. Passive scrolling and comparing oneself with others seems to be more harmful than actively communicating with friends. Using social media late at night appears to be especially damaging, because it disrupts sleep.

In response to public concern, some governments are considering or introducing new rules, such as minimum age limits for social media accounts or restrictions on phones in schools. Technology companies have introduced features designed to protect younger users. Meanwhile, many parents and teenagers are trying to find a healthier balance, deciding consciously when, why, and how they go online.

But every spring, when the apple tree blossoms, she finds herself glancing at the garden and smiling, just in case.

=== b2-the-economics-of-coffee | The Long Journey of Your Morning Coffee | Business | B2
For many people, the day does not really begin until they have had their first cup of coffee. Coffee is one of the most widely traded agricultural products in the world, and the global coffee industry is worth hundreds of billions of dollars every year. Yet the journey from a coffee farm to your cup is long and complex, and the money is shared very unequally along the way.

Most of the world's coffee is grown in a band of tropical countries near the equator, including Brazil, Vietnam, Colombia, Ethiopia, and Indonesia. Around twelve and a half million farms produce coffee, and the great majority of them are small family farms, often less than a few hectares in size. Coffee plants take several years to produce their first crop, and the fruit, known as coffee cherries, is often picked by hand.

After harvesting, the cherries are processed to remove the fruit and dry the seeds, which we call coffee beans. The beans are then sorted, packed, and shipped across the world, usually to be roasted in the countries where they will be consumed. Roasting transforms the green beans into the brown, aromatic beans we recognize, and it is at this stage, and later in cafes and shops, that much of the value is added.

According to some estimates, coffee farmers typically receive only a small fraction of the price a consumer pays for a cup of coffee in a cafe in Europe or North America. The rest goes to traders, roasters, retailers, and cafes. In addition, the price that farmers receive can vary dramatically from year to year, depending on the weather in major producing countries and the activity of financial markets. When prices fall, many small farmers struggle to cover their costs.

Climate change presents a further threat. Coffee plants, particularly the popular arabica variety, are sensitive to changes in temperature and rainfall. Some studies suggest that the area suitable for growing coffee could shrink significantly in the coming decades.

Various initiatives aim to make the coffee trade fairer and more sustainable, including fair trade certification, which guarantees farmers a minimum price, and direct trade relationships between roasters and farmers. Consumers who are willing to pay a little more for their coffee can help to support these efforts.

=== b2-the-surprising-history-of-the-sandwich | How the Sandwich Got Its Name | Food | B2
The sandwich is one of the most popular foods in the world. Every day, millions of people eat sandwiches for lunch, whether a simple combination of cheese and bread or an elaborate creation with many layers. It is so familiar that few people stop to wonder where it came from or why it has such an unusual name.

According to the most popular story, the sandwich was named after John Montagu, the fourth Earl of Sandwich, an English aristocrat who lived in the eighteenth century. The legend says that the earl was a passionate gambler who did not want to leave the card table to eat a proper meal. He asked his servants to bring him slices of meat between two pieces of bread, so that he could eat with one hand while continuing to play. Other gamblers began to ask for "the same as Sandwich," and the name stuck.

Historians, however, are not sure that this story is accurate. It comes mainly from a travel book written by a French author, who may have been repeating gossip. The earl's biographers have suggested that he was more likely eating at his desk while working, since he held a number of demanding government positions, including a role in charge of the navy.

What is certain is that the idea of eating food between pieces of bread is much older than the earl. People in many cultures had been doing so for centuries. In ancient times, a Jewish teacher is said to have eaten lamb and bitter herbs between pieces of flatbread during Passover. In medieval Europe, people often ate their food on thick slices of stale bread called trenchers, which served as plates. In the Middle East and India, flatbreads have long been used to wrap meat and vegetables.

What the earl did, perhaps, was to give a name and a fashionable reputation to a very old way of eating. Interestingly, the earl is also remembered in another way. The explorer James Cook named the Sandwich Islands, now known as Hawaii, in his honor.

Today, every culture seems to have its own version of the sandwich, from the Vietnamese banh mi to the Mexican torta and the Italian panini. It is a simple invention that has truly conquered the world.

=== b2-the-right-to-repair | The Right to Repair | Technology | B2
Have you ever had a smartphone with a battery that no longer lasted a full day, a washing machine that stopped working just after its warranty expired, or a printer that was cheaper to replace than to repair? If so, you are not alone. Many modern products are difficult or expensive to repair, and as a result, millions of devices are thrown away every year. A growing movement known as the right to repair is campaigning to change this.

Supporters of the right to repair argue that people who buy a product should have the ability to repair it themselves or take it to an independent repair shop of their choice. In practice, however, manufacturers often make this difficult. Some products are glued together, making it almost impossible to replace parts such as batteries without damaging the device. Spare parts, repair manuals, and special tools may not be available to the public. In some cases, devices contain software that prevents them from working properly if a part has been replaced by someone other than an authorized technician.

Manufacturers argue that these restrictions exist for good reasons. They say that repairs carried out by untrained people could be dangerous, for example if a battery is damaged and catches fire. They also want to protect their intellectual property and maintain the security and quality of their products.

Critics believe that the real motivation is often financial. If customers cannot easily repair their devices, they are more likely to pay high prices for official repairs or simply buy a new product. This contributes to a huge environmental problem. Electronic waste is one of the fastest-growing types of waste in the world, and only a small proportion of it is properly recycled. Producing new devices also requires large amounts of energy and raw materials, some of which are mined in difficult conditions.

The movement has achieved some important successes. The European Union and several American states have introduced laws requiring manufacturers to make spare parts and repair information available for a number of years. Some companies have started selling repair kits to the public. Many supporters believe this is only the beginning, and that in the future, products will be designed from the start to be repaired, upgraded, and reused.

=== b2-the-girl-who-fought-for-education | Malala's Voice | Society | B2
In October two thousand twelve, a fifteen-year-old girl named Malala Yousafzai was traveling home from school on a bus in the Swat Valley in northern Pakistan. A gunman stopped the bus, asked, "Who is Malala?" and then shot her in the head. Two of her friends were also injured. The attack shocked the world. But the girl the gunman had tried to silence would go on to become one of the most powerful voices for education on the planet.

Malala had grown up in a family that valued education. Her father ran a school, and she loved learning. However, in two thousand seven, an extremist group took control of much of the valley where she lived. They banned many activities, including watching television and listening to music, and they began destroying schools, particularly schools for girls. They announced that girls would no longer be allowed to attend school.

Malala refused to stay silent. At the age of eleven, she began writing an anonymous blog for a British news organization, describing her life under the extremists and her fear that she would not be able to continue her education. Later, she began to speak publicly, giving interviews to journalists and appearing on television. Her courage made her well known in Pakistan, and it also made her a target.

After the attack, Malala was flown to a hospital in Birmingham, in England, where she underwent several operations. Against the odds, she survived and recovered. Rather than being frightened into silence, she continued her campaign with even greater determination. Today, many millions of girls around the world are still out of school, because of poverty, conflict, discrimination, or early marriage. Malala's story is a reminder of both how much remains to be done and how much difference one determined person can make.

=== b2-the-science-of-cooking | The Chemistry in Your Kitchen | Science | B2
Every time we cook, we are performing a series of chemical experiments. Heat, acids, salt, and time transform the appearance, texture, and flavor of our ingredients in ways that can seem almost magical. Understanding a little of the science behind cooking can help explain why certain techniques work and can make us better cooks.

One of the most important chemical reactions in the kitchen is the Maillard reaction, named after the French scientist who first described it in the early twentieth century. When foods containing proteins and sugars are heated to high temperatures, they react to produce hundreds of new compounds that create brown colors and rich, savory flavors. The Maillard reaction is responsible for the delicious crust on a grilled steak, the golden color of toasted bread, and the aroma of roasted coffee. It happens most effectively when the surface of the food is dry, which is why chefs often recommend drying meat before frying it.

A different process, called caramelization, occurs when sugar itself is heated. As sugar breaks down, it turns brown and develops complex flavors that can be nutty, buttery, or slightly bitter. This is what happens when onions are cooked slowly until they become sweet and golden.

Eggs provide another fascinating example of kitchen chemistry. Egg whites contain proteins that are tightly folded. When eggs are heated, these proteins unfold and bond together, turning the liquid into a solid. When egg whites are whisked, air bubbles are trapped in a network of proteins, creating the light foam used in meringues and mousses.

Acids also play an important role. A squeeze of lemon juice can brighten the flavor of a dish, and acidic marinades can help to tenderize meat. In some dishes, such as ceviche, which is popular in Latin America, raw fish is "cooked" by soaking it in citrus juice, which changes the structure of its proteins without any heat at all.

Even salt does more than simply make food taste salty. It can reduce bitterness, enhance sweetness, and draw moisture out of vegetables. As many cooks know, the difference between a dull dish and a delicious one is often just a pinch of salt. Cooking, it seems, is both an art and a science.

=== b2-the-power-of-nudges | How Small Nudges Change Our Behavior | Psychology | B2
Governments and organizations have traditionally tried to change people's behavior in two main ways: by making rules and by offering financial rewards or punishments. If they want people to drive more safely, they introduce speed limits and fines. If they want people to save energy, they offer tax benefits. But in recent decades, a different approach has become increasingly popular. It is known as nudging.

The idea was popularized by the economist Richard Thaler and the legal scholar Cass Sunstein in a book published in two thousand eight. A nudge is a small change in the way choices are presented that encourages people to behave in a certain way, without forbidding any options or significantly changing financial incentives. The key insight is that human decisions are strongly influenced by context, habits, and mental shortcuts, often in ways we are not aware of.

One of the most famous examples involves retirement savings. In many countries, workers had to actively choose to join a pension plan, and many never did, even though it was in their interest. When some employers changed the system so that workers were automatically enrolled, unless they chose to leave, participation increased dramatically. People are strongly influenced by default options, because changing them requires effort.

Nudges can take many forms. Placing fruit at eye level in a school cafeteria and moving desserts to a less visible position can encourage children to eat more healthily. Sending letters to people who are late paying their taxes, telling them that most people in their area pay on time, has been shown to increase payments. Painting lines on roads that make drivers feel they are going faster can encourage them to slow down.

Several governments have set up special teams to apply these ideas to public policy. Supporters argue that nudges are cheap, effective, and respect people's freedom of choice.

However, nudging has also attracted criticism. Some people argue that it can be manipulative, because it influences people without their full awareness. Others point out that the same techniques can be used by companies to encourage people to spend more money or share personal data. There are also concerns that nudges are sometimes used as an easy alternative to more substantial measures. Like any tool, it seems, the power of nudging depends on how, and by whom, it is used.

=== b2-the-midnight-library-of-sounds | Recording a Disappearing World | Nature | B2
In the late nineteen sixties, an American musician and naturalist named Bernie Krause began recording the sounds of wild places. Over the following decades, he traveled to forests, deserts, wetlands, and oceans around the world, capturing thousands of hours of audio. His recordings form one of the largest archives of natural soundscapes ever created. But listening back to them today, Krause has noticed something deeply disturbing: many of the places he recorded have fallen silent.

Krause developed the idea that every healthy habitat has its own unique acoustic signature, which he called a biophony. Just as instruments in an orchestra play in different ranges so that each can be heard, he observed that different species tend to use different frequencies and time slots for their calls. Insects might occupy high frequencies, birds the middle range, and frogs and mammals lower sounds. Together, they create a complex, layered sound in which each species has its place.

When a habitat is damaged, this acoustic structure changes. In one striking example, Krause recorded a forest in California before and after a selective logging operation. Visually, the forest looked almost the same after the work, since only certain trees had been removed. Acoustically, however, it was transformed. The rich chorus of birds that he had previously recorded was reduced to a few scattered voices.

Krause estimates that a large percentage of the habitats in his archive are now either completely silent or so altered that their original soundscapes can no longer be heard. The causes include deforestation, agriculture, urban expansion, pollution, and climate change.

His work has inspired a growing field of research known as soundscape ecology. Scientists now use automated recorders placed in forests, reefs, and other habitats to monitor biodiversity over long periods. Listening to an ecosystem can reveal changes that might be missed by visual surveys, such as the return of a species after restoration or the gradual decline of insects.

Krause's recordings are also a reminder of what we stand to lose. For many listeners, hearing the voice of a vanished forest is a powerful emotional experience, more moving than any statistic. As he once said, a picture may be worth a thousand words, but a soundscape is worth a thousand pictures.

=== b2-the-four-minute-mile | Breaking the Four-Minute Barrier | Sport | B2
For much of the first half of the twentieth century, many athletes, coaches, and even doctors believed that it was physically impossible for a human being to run a mile, about one thousand six hundred meters, in less than four minutes. The best runners in the world had come close, but the barrier seemed unbreakable. Some experts warned that anyone who attempted it might seriously damage their health.

On the sixth of May, nineteen fifty-four, a twenty-five-year-old British medical student named Roger Bannister set out to prove them wrong. Bannister was an amateur athlete who trained around his demanding studies, often running for less than an hour a day. He approached the challenge scientifically, studying his own body and carefully planning his training.

The race took place at a small athletics track in Oxford. The weather was poor, with strong winds and rain, and Bannister almost decided not to run. Shortly before the start, the wind dropped. With the help of two friends who acted as pacemakers, setting a fast speed for the first laps, Bannister completed the four laps of the track. On the final lap, he ran alone, pushing himself to the limit.

When he crossed the line, he collapsed, almost unconscious. The crowd waited anxiously for the time to be announced. The announcer began, "The time was three," and the rest of his words were drowned out by the cheering of the crowd. Bannister had run the mile in three minutes and fifty-nine point four seconds.

What happened next is perhaps even more remarkable. Just forty-six days later, an Australian runner named John Landy broke Bannister's record. Within a few years, dozens of runners had run a mile in under four minutes. Today, the record stands at more than fifteen seconds faster than Bannister's time, and many high school students have run under four minutes.

The story is often used to illustrate the power of belief. Once athletes saw that the barrier could be broken, it no longer seemed impossible, and they were able to achieve the same result. Of course, improvements in training, tracks, and nutrition also played a role. But Bannister's achievement remains a powerful symbol of how human limits are often set as much by the mind as by the body.

=== b2-the-cost-of-convenience | The Hidden Cost of Next-Day Delivery | Society | B2
It has never been easier to shop. With a few taps on a smartphone, we can order almost anything, from books and clothes to groceries and furniture, and in many cities, it will arrive the next day or even within a few hours. For consumers, fast delivery is extremely convenient. But the systems that make it possible have significant costs that are often invisible to the people who benefit from them.

One of these costs is environmental. Fast delivery often means that vans travel with only partial loads, making more frequent trips to deliver individual packages to different addresses. This increases traffic, air pollution, and carbon emissions, particularly in urban areas. Many products are also wrapped in large amounts of packaging, much of which is not recycled. In addition, online shoppers tend to return a high proportion of their purchases, especially clothes, and some returned items are simply thrown away because it is cheaper than checking and repackaging them.

There are also human costs. Behind every quick delivery is a large workforce of warehouse employees and delivery drivers who work under intense pressure. In some warehouses, workers are expected to pick and pack hundreds of items per hour, and their movements are tracked by computer systems. Delivery drivers may have to complete a large number of deliveries in a single shift, leaving little time for breaks. Many are self-employed and lack the protections of regular employees.

Fast delivery also affects local communities. Small independent shops often struggle to compete with large online retailers that can offer lower prices and faster delivery. When local shops close, town centers can lose their character and their sense of community.

None of this means that online shopping is necessarily bad. For people who live far from shops, have limited mobility, or simply have busy lives, it can be invaluable. Some studies even suggest that well-organized delivery can produce lower emissions than many people driving to shops individually.

However, many experts believe that consumers could reduce some of the negative effects by making small changes. Choosing slower delivery options when speed is not necessary, combining orders, collecting parcels from local pickup points, and buying from local shops whenever possible can all help to make our shopping habits more sustainable.

=== b2-the-hundred-year-old-diary | A Diary from the Past | Short Fiction | B2
The diary had been hidden under a loose floorboard in the bedroom for almost a century. Ben found it by accident while replacing the old carpet in the house he had just bought. It was a small leather notebook, stiff and spotted with age, and on the first page, in careful handwriting, was written: "The private diary of Elsie Thornton, aged fourteen, nineteen twenty-four."

That evening, Ben sat on the floor of the empty room and began to read. Elsie wrote about her school, her strict father, her best friend Dora, and her dream of becoming a doctor, which her family considered ridiculous for a girl. She described the price of candy, the arrival of the first motor car in the street, and a boy called Albert who worked at the bakery and always gave her the largest bun.

As the months passed in the diary, the tone changed. Elsie's mother became ill, and Elsie had to leave school to look after her and her younger brothers. She wrote less often, and her entries became shorter and sadder. The last entry, written two years after the first, said only: "Mother died today. I will not be a doctor now. Perhaps one day someone else will be, for me."

Ben closed the diary and sat in silence for a long time. He could not stop thinking about the girl who had once slept in this room and whose dreams had been abandoned so young. Over the next few weeks, he began to search local records. He discovered that Elsie had married Albert from the bakery, had four children, and had lived in the same town until her death in nineteen eighty-six.

Eventually, he traced one of her granddaughters, a woman named Helen, who lived only an hour away. When Ben called her and explained what he had found, there was a long silence on the line. Then Helen began to cry. "My grandmother never talked about her childhood," she said. "And I am a doctor. I always wondered why she was so proud of me."

Ben gave Helen the diary. A few months later, he received a letter from her. Inside was a photograph of a small brass plaque, newly placed in the waiting room of the clinic where Helen worked. It read: "In memory of Elsie Thornton, who wanted to heal people."

=== b2-the-psychology-of-color | How Colors Affect Our Minds | Psychology | B2
Why are so many fast-food restaurants decorated in red and yellow? Why do banks often use blue in their logos? Why are hospital walls frequently painted in soft greens and pale blues? Many people believe that colors have a powerful influence on our emotions and behavior, and businesses spend considerable sums of money choosing exactly the right shades. But what does scientific research actually tell us about the psychology of color?

Some effects are well documented. Red, for example, tends to attract attention, which is one reason why it is used for warning signs, stop lights, and sale signs in shops. Several studies have suggested that red can increase arousal and may make people react more quickly. In sport, some research has found that athletes wearing red were slightly more likely to win in certain combat sports, perhaps because the color is associated with dominance and aggression, although these findings have been debated.

Blue, on the other hand, is often associated with calm, stability, and trust. It is consistently one of the most popular favorite colors in surveys across many different countries. This may explain why it is so common in the branding of banks, technology companies, and social media platforms, which want to appear reliable.

However, many popular claims about color are less well supported. The idea that painting a room a particular color can dramatically change people's mood or productivity, for example, has produced mixed results in research. One famous case involved a shade of pink that was claimed to reduce aggression in prisoners. Early reports were enthusiastic, but later studies failed to confirm the effect.

One of the main challenges is that the meaning of colors is strongly influenced by culture and personal experience. In many Western countries, white is associated with purity and weddings, while in parts of East Asia, it is traditionally associated with mourning and funerals. Red symbolizes danger in some contexts, but luck and celebration in China. A person who had a bad experience in a green room may feel differently about the color than someone who associates it with a happy childhood garden.

Context also matters. The same red that seems exciting on a sports car may feel alarming on a medical report. Psychologists therefore caution against simple rules. Color certainly affects us, but its effects are complex, subtle, and deeply shaped by where and how we encounter it.
