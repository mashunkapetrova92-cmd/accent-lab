=== b2-the-ethics-of-zoos | Should Zoos Still Exist? | Animals | B2
For many people, a visit to the zoo is a cherished childhood memory. Seeing an elephant, a lion, or a giraffe up close can be an unforgettable experience. Zoos attract hundreds of millions of visitors around the world every year. Yet in recent decades, a growing number of people have begun to question whether it is right to keep wild animals in captivity at all.

Critics of zoos argue that even the best enclosures cannot replicate the complexity of an animal's natural habitat. Elephants, for example, can walk many kilometers a day in the wild and live in large, close-knit family groups. Some marine mammals, such as orcas, travel enormous distances in the ocean. In captivity, some animals display repetitive behaviors, such as pacing back and forth or swaying, which scientists consider signs of stress or boredom. Critics also point out that many zoos still keep animals mainly for entertainment rather than for genuine conservation purposes.

Supporters of modern zoos, however, argue that the best institutions have changed dramatically. Many have replaced small cages with larger, more natural enclosures and provide activities to keep animals mentally and physically stimulated. They argue that zoos play a vital role in conservation. Through carefully managed breeding programs, zoos have helped to save several species from extinction, including the California condor and the Arabian oryx, both of which were later reintroduced into the wild.

Zoos also contribute to scientific research, providing opportunities to study animal behavior, health, and reproduction that would be difficult in the wild. Many fund conservation projects in the animals' native countries, protecting habitats and training local scientists. And supporters argue that by allowing people to see animals in person, zoos inspire visitors, particularly children, to care about wildlife and the environment.

Between these two positions, many experts take a middle view. They suggest that zoos should focus on species that can thrive in captivity and that genuinely benefit from conservation programs, while gradually phasing out the keeping of animals that suffer most, such as elephants and large marine mammals. Some argue that resources should increasingly be directed toward protecting animals in their natural habitats, perhaps through sanctuaries and wildlife reserves.

The debate is likely to continue. What is clear is that the role of zoos is changing, and that the question of how humans should share the planet with other species has never been more urgent.

=== b2-the-art-of-the-handwritten-note | The Lost Art of Letter Writing | Culture | B2
There was a time when letters were the main way that people separated by distance kept in touch. Families exchanged news across oceans, lovers poured their feelings onto paper, and friendships were maintained over decades through regular correspondence. Today, most of us communicate through instant messages and emails, and many young people have never written or received a personal letter. Something valuable, some argue, has been lost.

Letters have played an important role in history. Much of what we know about the lives and thoughts of famous people comes from their correspondence. The letters of writers, artists, scientists, and politicians reveal their private doubts, their humor, and their relationships in ways that official documents cannot. The painter Vincent van Gogh, for example, wrote hundreds of letters to his brother Theo, which provide extraordinary insight into his art and his struggles with mental illness.

Ordinary people's letters are equally precious. Historians use letters written by soldiers, immigrants, and workers to understand how people experienced major events. A letter from a soldier to his mother during a war can communicate the reality of that experience more powerfully than any history book.

Part of the special value of a letter lies in its physical nature. Handwriting is personal and unique. A letter can be held, kept in a drawer, and reread years later. Many people treasure letters from parents or grandparents long after they have died, finding comfort in the familiar shape of their writing.

Letter writing also encourages a different kind of communication. Because writing by hand takes time and effort, people tend to think more carefully about what they want to say. Letters are often longer, more reflective, and more personal than digital messages. The delay between sending and receiving a letter also creates a sense of anticipation that instant messaging cannot match.

Interestingly, there are signs of a small revival. Some people have rediscovered the pleasure of writing letters, joining pen pal programs or sending handwritten cards to friends. Research suggests that receiving a handwritten thank you note has a much greater emotional impact than people expect. In a world of constant digital communication, a letter in the mailbox may have become more special than ever.

=== b2-the-volcano-that-changed-the-weather | The Year Without a Summer | History | B2
In April eighteen fifteen, Mount Tambora, a volcano on the island of Sumbawa in what is now Indonesia, erupted with extraordinary violence. It was the most powerful volcanic eruption in recorded history. The explosions could be heard more than two thousand kilometers away, and the top of the mountain was blown off, reducing its height by more than a kilometer. Tens of thousands of people on the surrounding islands were killed, either directly by the eruption or by the starvation and disease that followed.

But the effects of Tambora were not limited to Indonesia. The eruption threw enormous quantities of ash and gas high into the atmosphere. Among these gases was sulfur dioxide, which formed tiny droplets that spread around the globe and reflected some of the sun's light back into space. As a result, global temperatures fell.

The following year, eighteen sixteen, became known in Europe and North America as "the year without a summer." In parts of the northeastern United States, snow fell in June, and frosts continued throughout the summer, destroying crops. In Europe, constant rain and cold temperatures led to failed harvests. Food prices rose dramatically, and many people faced hunger. There were riots in several countries, and thousands of people emigrated in search of better conditions.

The strange weather also had some unexpected cultural consequences. In the summer of eighteen sixteen, a group of young English writers was staying in a villa near Lake Geneva in Switzerland. Because of the constant storms, they were forced to spend much of their time indoors. To entertain themselves, they decided to hold a competition to see who could write the most frightening story. One of them, eighteen-year-old Mary Shelley, began writing a story that would become the novel "Frankenstein," one of the most famous works of horror fiction ever written.

Some historians have also suggested that the dramatic, colorful sunsets painted by artists during this period may have been influenced by the volcanic particles in the atmosphere.

At the time, nobody connected the bad weather with a volcano on the other side of the world. It was only in the twentieth century that scientists understood the link. Today, the story of Tambora is studied as a powerful example of how events in one part of the world can affect the climate and lives of people everywhere.

=== b2-the-unexpected-friendship | Letters to a Prisoner | Short Fiction | B2
It began with a mistake. Grace, a seventy-eight-year-old widow who lived alone in a small town, had signed up for a pen pal program at her local library, expecting to correspond with a student overseas. Instead, due to a mix-up with the forms, she received a letter from a man named Marcus, who was serving a twelve-year sentence in a prison three hundred kilometers away.

Her first instinct was to throw the letter away. Her daughter, when she heard about it, told her to do exactly that. "You don't know what he did," she said. "He could be dangerous." Over the following months, their letters grew longer. Marcus told her about the books he was reading in the prison library and the courses he had begun to take. Grace sent him newspaper clippings and, once, a pressed flower from her garden.

He wrote that he thought about that man every day and did not expect her to forgive him. Grace read the letter many times. Then she wrote back: "I am not the person you need to ask for forgiveness. He was nervous, holding a bunch of flowers, unsure if she would want to see him in person. She opened the door, looked at him for a long moment, and then invited him in for tea.

Marcus now works as a gardener and volunteers with a charity that supports prisoners returning to society. He visits Grace every Sunday. Her daughter, who was so worried at the beginning, now says that Marcus is the most reliable person she knows.

=== b2-the-power-of-trees-in-cities | Why Cities Need Trees | Environment | B2
As cities grow, they tend to become warmer than the surrounding countryside. Concrete, asphalt, and brick absorb heat during the day and release it slowly at night, while cars, air conditioners, and factories produce additional heat. This phenomenon, known as the urban heat island effect, can make cities several degrees hotter than nearby rural areas. As the climate changes and heatwaves become more frequent and intense, finding ways to cool cities has become an urgent priority. One of the simplest and most effective solutions is to plant more trees.

Trees cool their surroundings in two main ways. First, they provide shade, preventing sunlight from heating streets, buildings, and people. Second, they release water vapor through their leaves, a process that cools the air around them, much like sweating cools the human body. Studies have shown that streets with plenty of trees can be significantly cooler on hot summer days than streets without them.

The benefits of urban trees go far beyond cooling. Trees absorb carbon dioxide and can help to filter some pollutants from the air. Their roots absorb rainwater, reducing the risk of flooding during heavy storms. They provide homes and food for birds, insects, and other wildlife, increasing biodiversity in urban areas.

Trees also appear to have significant benefits for human health and well-being. Research has linked access to green spaces with lower levels of stress, better mental health, and increased physical activity. In one well-known study, hospital patients who had a view of trees from their window recovered more quickly after surgery and needed fewer painkillers than patients whose windows faced a brick wall. Some studies have even found that neighborhoods with more trees have lower crime rates, although the reasons are complex.

Despite these benefits, trees in cities face many challenges. Space is limited, roots can damage sidewalks and pipes, and trees can be expensive to plant and maintain. In many cities, wealthier neighborhoods have many more trees than poorer ones, which means that the residents who most need cooling often have the least access to it.

Many cities have now launched ambitious programs to plant hundreds of thousands or even millions of new trees. Experts emphasize that it is important not only to plant trees, but to choose the right species, plant them in the right places, and care for them over the long term.

=== b2-the-woman-who-flew-alone | Amelia Earhart's Final Flight | History | B2
In the nineteen twenties and thirties, aviation was still a new and dangerous adventure, and pilots who set records became international celebrities. Among the most famous was Amelia Earhart, an American who broke barriers not only in the sky but also in the way society viewed women.

Earhart took her first flight in nineteen twenty, as a passenger in a small plane at an air show in California. By the time she landed, she later said, she knew she had to fly. She took lessons, worked several jobs to pay for them, and bought her first airplane. In nineteen thirty-two, she became the first woman to fly alone across the Atlantic Ocean, a dangerous journey of almost fifteen hours during which she faced strong winds, ice on her wings, and mechanical problems.

Earhart used her fame to promote aviation and to encourage women to pursue careers that were considered unsuitable for them. She wrote books, gave lectures across the country, and helped to found an organization for female pilots. She believed that women should have the same opportunities as men in every field.

In nineteen thirty-seven, she set out on her most ambitious challenge yet: to become the first woman to fly around the world near the equator. With her navigator, Fred Noonan, she flew from California across the United States, South America, Africa, and Asia. After about a month, they had completed more than two thirds of the journey and arrived in New Guinea.

The next stage was one of the most difficult: a flight of more than four thousand kilometers across the Pacific Ocean to Howland Island, a tiny piece of land barely two kilometers long. On the second of July, they took off. Many hours later, a ship waiting near the island received radio messages from Earhart saying that they could not see the island and were running low on fuel. Then the messages stopped.

Despite one of the largest search operations in history, neither Earhart nor Noonan, nor their plane, was ever found. Their disappearance remains one of the great mysteries of the twentieth century. Some believe they crashed into the ocean and sank. Others think they may have landed on another remote island and survived for a time. Expeditions continue to search for answers. Whatever happened, Earhart remains a powerful symbol of courage and independence.

=== b2-the-benefits-of-failure | Why Schools Should Teach Failure | Education | B2
In many education systems, success is measured by grades, test scores, and rankings. Students are rewarded for getting the right answers and penalized for mistakes. As a result, many young people grow up deeply afraid of failure. They avoid difficult challenges, choose subjects they know they will do well in, and feel devastated when they receive a disappointing mark. Some educators now argue that schools should do more to teach students how to fail, and how to learn from failure.

Research on learning suggests that mistakes are not only inevitable but essential. When we make a mistake and then correct it, we often remember the correct information better than if we had gotten it right the first time. Some studies have found that students who struggle with a difficult problem before being shown the solution understand the topic more deeply than students who are simply taught the solution directly. Psychologists call these "desirable difficulties."

The way students think about failure also matters. As psychologist Carol Dweck has shown in her research on mindsets, students who believe that intelligence can grow with effort are more likely to persevere when they face difficulties. Students who believe that intelligence is fixed tend to see failure as proof that they are not smart, and they may give up more quickly.

Some schools have begun to introduce activities designed to change students' attitudes toward failure. Teachers may share stories of their own mistakes, celebrate interesting errors in class, or give students challenging projects in which there is no single correct answer. Some schools have even held "failure weeks," during which students and staff discuss their failures openly and reflect on what they learned.

Critics caution that such approaches must be handled carefully. Simply telling students that failure is good can feel hollow, especially for students who face real consequences when they fail important exams. Learning from failure requires support, feedback, and opportunities to try again. Without these, failure can simply be discouraging.

Nevertheless, many employers say that resilience, creativity, and the ability to learn from mistakes are among the most important skills they look for. In a rapidly changing world, where many problems have no easy solutions, the ability to fail well may be as valuable as any academic knowledge.

=== b2-the-secret-language-of-elephants | How Elephants Communicate | Animals | B2
Elephants are among the most intelligent and social animals on Earth. They live in complex family groups led by an older female, known as the matriarch, who guides the group to food and water and protects its members from danger. To maintain these close relationships, elephants rely on a sophisticated system of communication, much of which humans cannot hear.

Elephants produce a wide range of sounds, including trumpets, roars, barks, and rumbles. Many of their rumbles are at frequencies so low that they are below the range of human hearing. These low-frequency sounds, known as infrasound, can travel several kilometers, allowing elephants to stay in contact with members of their group even when they are far apart, or to find mates over long distances.

Remarkably, elephants may also be able to detect vibrations through the ground. Researchers believe that elephants can sense low-frequency rumbles traveling through the earth with sensitive cells in their feet and trunks. This could allow them to receive messages from distant elephants or detect approaching danger, such as a herd running away from predators.

Elephants also communicate through touch, using their trunks to greet, comfort, and reassure each other. They have been observed caressing distressed companions and wrapping their trunks together in what appears to be a gesture of affection. Scent plays an important role too, providing information about an elephant's identity and condition.

In recent years, scientists have made an even more surprising discovery. A study of wild African elephants in Kenya found evidence that elephants may address each other with unique calls, something like individual names. When researchers played recordings of calls originally directed at a specific elephant, that elephant responded more strongly than when it heard calls directed at others. Very few animals are known to do this, and it suggests that elephants may be capable of abstract thought.

Elephants are also known for their remarkable memory. Matriarchs remember the locations of water sources during droughts and can recognize the calls of many other elephants. They have also been observed behaving in ways that suggest they mourn their dead, touching and staying near the bodies of family members.

Understanding how elephants communicate is not only fascinating but also important for their conservation. As human settlements expand, conflicts between people and elephants are increasing. Knowledge of elephant behavior can help to design better ways of protecting both.

=== b2-the-surprising-science-of-smell | The Forgotten Sense | Science | B2
Among our five senses, smell is often considered the least important. Many people say that if they had to lose one sense, smell is the one they would choose. Yet scientists are increasingly discovering that our sense of smell is far more powerful and influential than we tend to believe.

For many years, it was widely claimed that humans could distinguish only around ten thousand different smells. This number was based on an old estimate that was never properly tested. In a study published in two thousand fourteen, researchers suggested that humans may in fact be able to distinguish more than a trillion different odors. While this figure has also been debated, it is clear that our noses are much more sensitive than previously thought.

Smell is closely connected to the parts of the brain that process emotion and memory. Unlike our other senses, information about smells travels almost directly to these regions. This is why a particular smell, such as freshly baked bread or a certain perfume, can instantly bring back vivid memories from many years ago, often accompanied by strong emotions. This phenomenon is sometimes called the Proust effect, after the French writer who described how the taste and smell of a small cake dipped in tea brought back memories of his childhood.

Smell also plays a crucial role in taste. Much of what we think of as flavor actually comes from our sense of smell. When we have a cold and our nose is blocked, food often tastes bland. Without smell, we can still detect basic tastes such as sweet, salty, sour, and bitter, but we lose the rich complexity of flavors.

The importance of smell became particularly clear during the recent pandemic, when millions of people temporarily or permanently lost their sense of smell. Many reported that the experience had a much greater effect on their lives than they had expected. They lost enjoyment of food, felt disconnected from their surroundings, and some even experienced depression. They also became more vulnerable to dangers such as gas leaks, smoke, and spoiled food.

Researchers are now exploring whether changes in our sense of smell might help to detect diseases early. Some studies suggest that a decline in the ability to smell can be an early sign of conditions such as Parkinson's disease. Far from being a minor sense, smell may offer a valuable window into our health and our minds.

=== b2-the-workplace-of-the-future | Will Robots Take Our Jobs? | Work | B2
Throughout history, new technologies have transformed the world of work. The invention of the steam engine, electricity, and computers all destroyed some jobs while creating many new ones. Today, advances in artificial intelligence and robotics are raising the question once again: will machines take our jobs?

Some studies have produced alarming predictions. A widely cited study from the University of Oxford in two thousand thirteen estimated that nearly half of jobs in the United States were at high risk of being automated in the coming decades. Since then, the rapid development of AI systems that can write texts, generate images, and analyze data has led some experts to argue that even highly skilled professions may be affected.

The jobs most at risk are generally those that involve routine, predictable tasks. Machines are already widely used in factories, and automated systems now handle many customer service inquiries. Self-checkout machines have reduced the need for cashiers in supermarkets. AI tools can now draft legal documents, translate texts, and assist with medical diagnoses, tasks that were once thought to require human expertise.

However, many economists believe that the future is less bleak than these predictions suggest. They point out that automation often changes jobs rather than eliminating them entirely. When cash machines were introduced, many people expected bank tellers to disappear. Instead, the number of tellers actually increased for many years, because banks opened more branches and tellers spent more time on customer service. Technology can also create entirely new kinds of jobs. Few people twenty years ago could have imagined careers such as social media manager or app developer.

There is broad agreement that skills such as creativity, critical thinking, empathy, and complex communication will become increasingly valuable, because they are difficult for machines to replicate. Jobs that involve caring for people, such as nursing and teaching, are also likely to remain in demand.

The greatest challenge may be ensuring that the transition is fair. Workers whose jobs are automated may need support to retrain and find new employment. Some experts have proposed ideas such as lifelong learning programs and even a universal basic income. How societies respond to these changes will determine whether new technologies lead to greater prosperity for everyone or to rising inequality.

=== b2-the-origins-of-writing | How Writing Began | History | B2
Writing is one of the most important inventions in human history. It allows us to record information, communicate across distances and time, and preserve knowledge for future generations. Without it, there would be no history books, no laws written down, and no scientific papers. Yet for most of the roughly three hundred thousand years that our species has existed, humans did not write at all. So how did writing begin?

The earliest known writing system developed in Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq, more than five thousand years ago. It did not begin with poetry or stories, but with accounting. As cities grew and trade expanded, people needed a way to keep track of goods such as grain, livestock, and beer. At first, they used small clay tokens of different shapes to represent different items. Later, they began making marks on clay tablets.

Over time, these marks evolved into a writing system known as cuneiform, from the Latin word for "wedge," because the symbols were made by pressing a sharpened reed into wet clay, creating wedge-shaped marks. At first, symbols were mostly pictures representing objects. Gradually, they came to represent sounds and syllables as well, which allowed people to write names, abstract ideas, and eventually literature. One of the oldest surviving works of literature, the Epic of Gilgamesh, was written in cuneiform.

Writing appears to have been invented independently in several other places. In Egypt, hieroglyphs developed around the same time as cuneiform. In China, writing appeared more than three thousand years ago, and modern Chinese characters are descended from these early forms. In Central America, the Maya developed a sophisticated writing system that was used to record history, astronomy, and religious ceremonies.

Most of the alphabets used today, including the Latin, Greek, Arabic, and Hebrew alphabets, are descended from a system developed by the Phoenicians, a trading people of the eastern Mediterranean, around three thousand years ago. The Phoenician alphabet used a small number of symbols to represent individual sounds, making writing much easier to learn than systems with hundreds or thousands of symbols.

For thousands of years, however, literacy remained the privilege of a small elite, such as priests, scribes, and officials. It was only in the last two centuries that the ability to read and write became widespread. Today, the large majority of adults in the world can read and write, a remarkable change in a relatively short time.

=== b2-the-power-of-daydreaming | In Defense of Daydreaming | Psychology | B2
Most of us were told as children to stop daydreaming and pay attention. Daydreaming has traditionally been seen as a waste of time, a sign of laziness, or a failure to concentrate. And there is some truth to this. Studies suggest that we spend almost half of our waking hours thinking about something other than what we are doing, and in some situations, such as driving or listening to instructions, a wandering mind can cause problems. Yet researchers are increasingly discovering that daydreaming also has important benefits.

When our minds wander, a network of brain regions known as the default mode network becomes particularly active. This network was originally discovered when scientists noticed that certain areas of the brain became more active when people were resting rather than performing tasks. It is now believed to be involved in thinking about ourselves, remembering the past, imagining the future, and understanding other people.

One of the most important benefits of daydreaming may be creativity. Many people report having their best ideas when they are not actively trying to solve a problem, for example while taking a shower, going for a walk, or lying in bed. In one study, participants who were given a boring task that allowed their minds to wander performed better afterward on a creative problem-solving test than participants who had done a demanding task or no task at all.

Daydreaming also helps us plan for the future. When our minds wander, we often think about upcoming events, rehearse conversations, and consider different possibilities. This kind of mental time travel can help us set goals and prepare for challenges. Some researchers believe that daydreaming also plays a role in consolidating memories and making sense of our experiences.

However, not all daydreaming is beneficial. When mind wandering takes the form of repetitive negative thoughts, such as dwelling on worries or past mistakes, it is associated with lower mood and anxiety. The content of our daydreams seems to matter. Positive, constructive daydreams about the future or interesting ideas are more likely to make us feel good than anxious or self-critical ones.

In a world where smartphones fill every spare moment with information and entertainment, some psychologists worry that we are losing opportunities to let our minds wander. They suggest that occasionally putting away our devices and allowing ourselves to daydream could make us more creative, more reflective, and perhaps even happier.

=== b2-the-fight-for-clean-air | The City That Cleaned Its Air | Environment | B2
For many years, London was famous for its fog. In December nineteen fifty-two, a combination of cold weather, still air, and smoke from millions of coal fires created a thick, yellow-black smog that covered the city for five days. Visibility was so poor that people could not see their own feet. Buses stopped running, and performances at theaters were canceled because audiences could not see the stage. The Great Smog, as it became known, is estimated to have caused thousands of deaths, mainly from breathing problems, with some studies suggesting the true number was much higher.

The disaster shocked the public and led to major changes. In nineteen fifty-six, the British government passed the Clean Air Act, which restricted the burning of coal in homes and factories in cities and encouraged the use of cleaner fuels. Over the following decades, air quality improved dramatically, and the famous London fog gradually disappeared.

Today, the main source of air pollution in many cities is no longer coal fires but road traffic. Vehicle exhaust contains pollutants such as nitrogen dioxide and tiny particles that can penetrate deep into the lungs and even enter the bloodstream. Air pollution has been linked to a wide range of health problems, including asthma, heart disease, strokes, and lung cancer. The World Health Organization estimates that air pollution is responsible for millions of premature deaths around the world every year.

Cities have tried various approaches to tackle the problem. London introduced a congestion charge in two thousand three, requiring drivers to pay a fee to enter the city center during busy hours. Later, it created an Ultra Low Emission Zone, in which owners of older, more polluting vehicles must pay a daily charge. These measures have been controversial, with some residents and businesses arguing that they are unfair to people who cannot afford newer vehicles. However, official data suggest that they have significantly reduced levels of some pollutants.

Other cities have taken different approaches. Paris has closed some roads near the river to cars and turned them into spaces for walking and cycling. Many cities are investing in electric buses, expanding public transportation, and building bike lanes.

The history of London's air shows that pollution is not inevitable. With political will and public support, cities can become cleaner and healthier places to live. The challenge is to ensure that the benefits and costs are shared fairly.

=== b2-the-art-forger | The Man Who Fooled the Experts | Culture | B2
In the years after the Second World War, a Dutch painter named Han van Meegeren became the center of one of the most extraordinary stories in the history of art. Arrested in nineteen forty-five, he was accused of a serious crime: selling a valuable national treasure, a painting by the seventeenth-century Dutch master Johannes Vermeer, to the senior Nazi official Hermann Goering. If convicted of collaborating with the enemy, he could face the death penalty.

Van Meegeren's defense was astonishing. He claimed that he had not sold a Vermeer to Goering at all. The painting, he said, was a fake, and he had painted it himself. In fact, he said, he had painted several "Vermeers" that had been sold for huge sums and were now hanging in major museums. Nobody believed him. The paintings had been examined and authenticated by leading experts, including one of the most respected Vermeer scholars in the world.

To prove his claim, van Meegeren offered to paint a new "Vermeer" in front of witnesses. Over several weeks, under supervision, he produced a painting in Vermeer's style using the same methods he claimed to have used for his forgeries. He had developed a technique that made new paintings appear centuries old, using old canvases, special pigments, and a chemical that hardened the paint when heated in an oven, creating the fine cracks typical of old paintings.

The experts were forced to accept the truth. Van Meegeren became, to some, a national hero: the man who had fooled the Nazis. The charge of collaboration was dropped, and instead he was convicted of forgery and fraud. He was sentenced to one year in prison, but he died of a heart attack before he could serve his sentence.

His story raises fascinating questions about art and value. Van Meegeren had begun his career as a painter but was dismissed by critics as old-fashioned and unoriginal. Some believe he turned to forgery partly out of a desire for revenge against the experts who had rejected him. Interestingly, many modern viewers find his forgeries rather unconvincing, and wonder how the experts could have been fooled. Part of the answer may be that people saw what they wanted to see: the experts were eager to discover a new Vermeer and were blinded by their own expectations.

=== b2-the-seed-vault | The Doomsday Vault | Science | B2
On a remote island in the Arctic Ocean, about one thousand three hundred kilometers from the North Pole, a tunnel leads deep into the side of a frozen mountain. At the end of the tunnel, behind several heavy doors, lies one of the most important storage facilities in the world. It does not contain gold or secret documents, but seeds. More than a million samples of seeds, from almost every country on Earth, are stored there at a temperature of minus eighteen degrees Celsius.

The Svalbard Global Seed Vault opened in two thousand eight on the Norwegian island of Spitsbergen. Its purpose is to protect the diversity of the world's food crops. Over thousands of years, farmers have developed countless varieties of plants, each adapted to particular conditions. Some varieties of wheat or rice may be resistant to certain diseases, while others can survive drought, heat, or salty soil. This genetic diversity is a vital resource for the future, especially as the climate changes and new diseases appear.

Around the world, there are many seed banks that store samples of crops. However, these banks can be vulnerable to wars, natural disasters, equipment failures, and lack of funding. The Svalbard vault acts as a backup for these collections. Countries and organizations deposit copies of their seeds there, and they remain the owners. Only they can withdraw them.

The location was chosen carefully. The surrounding permafrost keeps the vault cold even if the electricity fails. The vault is built high enough above sea level to remain dry even if the polar ice caps melt. The island is also politically stable and remote.

In two thousand fifteen, the vault was used for the first time. During the civil war in Syria, an important seed bank near the city of Aleppo could no longer operate. Researchers requested the return of the seeds they had deposited in Svalbard, and used them to start new collections in Lebanon and Morocco.

However, the vault has not been completely free from problems. In two thousand sixteen, unusually high temperatures caused melting permafrost to flood the entrance tunnel. The seeds were not damaged, but the incident showed that even the Arctic is not immune to climate change. The entrance has since been improved. For many people, the vault is a symbol of both humanity's vulnerability and its ability to plan for the future.

=== b2-the-perfect-crime | The Diamond Necklace | Short Fiction | B2
The Countess Valentina Orlova held her famous New Year's Eve party every year at her villa overlooking the lake. Fifty guests, champagne, an orchestra, and, at the center of it all, the Countess herself, wearing the Orlov necklace, a string of diamonds said to be worth several million dollars. At eleven forty-five, she went upstairs to change her shoes. At midnight, when she returned to the ballroom, the necklace was gone.

Inspector Clara Novak arrived just before one o'clock. She found the Countess in tears, the guests nervous and irritated, and the doors of the villa locked on her instructions. "Nobody has left," the butler assured her. "The gates have been closed since ten o'clock."

The Countess explained that she had taken off the necklace in her bedroom and placed it in a velvet box on her dressing table. She had been gone for no more than ten minutes, but during that time, three people had come upstairs. Her nephew, Viktor, said he had gone to the bathroom. Her secretary, Ms. Hale, said she had been fetching the Countess's medicine. And a famous pianist, Leon Marchetti, said he had simply gotten lost looking for the terrace.

Inspector Novak questioned each of them carefully. Viktor was nervous and admitted he had large gambling debts. Ms. Hale was calm and precise. Marchetti was charming but vague. All three agreed to be searched, and the necklace was not found on any of them. The guests began to grumble that the inspector was wasting their time.

Then Novak asked a question nobody expected. "Countess," she said, "when you came back to the ballroom at midnight, the orchestra was playing. What were they playing?" The Countess looked surprised. "A waltz, I think. Why?" Novak turned to the pianist. "Mr. Marchetti, you told me you heard the orchestra begin the countdown to midnight while you were on the terrace. But the musicians tell me that at midnight they were taking a break. The only music playing was a recording."

Marchetti's smile faded. "The recording," Novak continued, "was on a device that you yourself provided for the party. You made sure the orchestra would be away from the stairs." The necklace was later found inside the pianist's music case, hidden in the lining. As the police led him away, the Countess shook her head. "And I paid him to play," she said.

=== b2-the-hidden-cost-of-cheap-flights | The True Cost of Flying | Travel | B2
In recent decades, air travel has become accessible to millions of people who would once have considered it an unimaginable luxury. Thanks to low-cost airlines, it is now often possible to fly across a continent for less than the price of a train ticket. Cheap flights have opened up the world, allowing people to visit family, discover new cultures, and take vacations in distant places. However, they also have significant environmental consequences.

Aviation is one of the most carbon-intensive ways to travel. Although it currently accounts for a relatively small share of global carbon dioxide emissions, compared with sectors such as energy and road transport, its impact on the climate is greater than this figure suggests. Planes release emissions high in the atmosphere, where other gases and the white trails left by aircraft, called contrails, can increase their warming effect.

The distribution of these emissions is also highly unequal. Most of the world's population has never taken a flight, while a small percentage of frequent flyers are responsible for a large share of aviation emissions. A single round-trip flight between Europe and North America can produce more emissions per passenger than many people in poorer countries produce in a whole year.

Reducing emissions from aviation is particularly challenging. Unlike cars, which can increasingly be powered by electricity, large passenger planes require enormous amounts of energy, and current batteries are too heavy to power long-distance flights. The industry is investing in alternatives, such as sustainable aviation fuels made from waste materials or plants, and experimental aircraft powered by hydrogen. However, these technologies are still expensive and are not expected to be widely available for many years.

Some governments have introduced or considered taxes on flights, particularly for frequent flyers or short routes that could be replaced by trains. France, for example, has banned some short domestic flights when a train journey of less than two and a half hours is available. Critics argue that such measures could make flying unaffordable for ordinary families while having little effect on wealthy travelers.

For individuals, experts suggest considering alternatives such as trains for shorter journeys, flying less often but staying longer, and choosing direct flights, which tend to produce fewer emissions than flights with connections. Travel can be one of life's great pleasures, but as with many aspects of modern life, it may be time to think more carefully about its true cost.

=== b2-the-lost-art-of-boredom-in-childhood | Let Children Be Bored | Education | B2
Many modern parents feel enormous pressure to keep their children busy. After school, children may attend music lessons, sports practice, language classes, and tutoring sessions. At home, screens offer endless entertainment. When a child says, "I'm bored," many parents rush to find something for them to do. But some child development experts believe that a little boredom may actually be good for children.

According to these experts, boredom can stimulate creativity and imagination. When children have nothing specific to do, they are forced to entertain themselves. They might invent games, build forts from cushions, write stories, or explore the garden. These activities, which are not organized or directed by adults, help children develop problem-solving skills and independence. Some researchers argue that unstructured play is essential for healthy development.

Boredom may also teach children to tolerate frustration and to manage their own emotions. Life inevitably includes moments that are not exciting, such as waiting in line or doing routine chores. Children who are always entertained may find these moments very difficult to cope with. Learning to handle boredom can help them develop patience and self-control.

There are concerns that today's children have fewer opportunities to experience boredom than previous generations. Many spend a large part of their free time in organized activities or in front of screens. Some studies suggest that the amount of time children spend in free, unsupervised play has declined significantly over recent decades, partly because parents are worried about safety.

Of course, this does not mean that structured activities or screens are bad. Music lessons, sports, and educational programs can offer valuable experiences and skills. The problem arises when every moment of a child's day is filled, leaving no time for them to discover their own interests.

Experts suggest that parents can encourage healthy boredom by setting aside some unscheduled time each week, limiting screen use, and resisting the urge to solve every moment of boredom for their children. Providing simple materials, such as paper, crayons, boxes, or building blocks, can give children the tools to create their own entertainment. As one psychologist put it, boredom is the space where children learn to listen to themselves.

=== b2-the-language-detective | How Linguists Decoded Ancient Egypt | History | B2
For more than a thousand years, nobody in the world could read the hieroglyphs of ancient Egypt. The knowledge had been lost after the last people who could write them died out, around the fifth century. Travelers and scholars admired the beautiful symbols carved on temples and monuments, but they could only guess at their meaning. Many believed the symbols were magical or mystical images rather than a form of writing that represented language.

The key to the mystery was discovered in seventeen ninety-nine, when French soldiers, part of Napoleon's military expedition to Egypt, found a large stone slab near the town of Rashid, known to Europeans as Rosetta. The stone was covered in writing in three different scripts: hieroglyphs, a later Egyptian script called demotic, and ancient Greek. Scholars quickly realized that the three sections probably contained the same text. Since ancient Greek was well understood, the Rosetta Stone offered a chance to finally unlock the hieroglyphs.

Even so, it took more than twenty years to succeed. Several scholars made important progress. The English scientist Thomas Young identified some of the symbols that represented sounds, particularly in royal names, which were written inside oval shapes called cartouches. But it was a young French linguist named Jean-Francois Champollion who achieved the breakthrough.

Champollion had been fascinated by ancient Egypt since childhood and had learned many languages, including Coptic, a language descended from ancient Egyptian that was still used in Christian religious ceremonies in Egypt. This knowledge proved crucial. In eighteen twenty-two, after years of work, Champollion realized that hieroglyphs were not purely pictures, nor purely an alphabet, but a complex combination of both. Some symbols represented sounds, while others represented whole words or ideas. According to a popular story, when he made his discovery, he ran to his brother's office, shouted "I've done it!" and fainted.

Champollion's work opened up an entire civilization. Scholars could now read inscriptions on temples, tombs, and papyrus documents, revealing the history, religion, literature, and daily life of ancient Egypt in astonishing detail. Today, the Rosetta Stone is one of the most visited objects in the British Museum in London, although Egypt has repeatedly asked for its return. It remains a powerful symbol of the human desire to understand the past.
