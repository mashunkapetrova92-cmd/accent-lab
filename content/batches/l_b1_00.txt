=== b1-the-four-day-week | Is a Four-Day Week the Future? | Work | B1
For more than a hundred years, most office workers have worked five days a week. But in recent years, some companies have started to try something different: a four-day week. Employees work four days instead of five, but they receive the same salary.

At first, this sounds like a bad idea for businesses. If people work less, surely they will produce less? However, the results of several experiments have been surprising. In many companies, employees got the same amount of work done in four days. They spent less time in long meetings, took fewer breaks, and concentrated better because they knew they had a three-day weekend.

Workers in these experiments also reported many personal benefits. They felt less stressed and slept better. They had more time for their families, hobbies, and exercise. Some said they finally had time to do everyday jobs, like going to the doctor or repairing things at home, without taking a day off work.

Of course, the four-day week doesn't work for every job. Hospitals, schools, and shops need people every day of the week, so they would need to hire more staff. Some critics also worry that people will try to fit five days of work into four, and that they will end up working very long, exhausting days.

Still, the idea is becoming more popular. If the experiments continue to be successful, it is possible that in twenty years, a five-day week will seem as old-fashioned as working on Saturdays does today.

=== b1-the-man-who-planted-trees | The Man Who Planted Trees | Environment | B1
In the late nineteen seventies, a teenager called Jadav Payeng was living near a large river in northeast India. Every year, the river flooded and washed away the land on a big sandy island in the middle of it. After one terrible flood, Jadav found hundreds of dead snakes on the sand. There were no trees to protect them from the heat.

The scene shocked him. He asked the older people in his village what he could do, and they told him to plant trees. So he did. He started with twenty small bamboo plants. Then he planted more, and more. Every day for the next forty years, he went to the island to plant and water trees.

Slowly, the empty sand became a forest. Today, the forest covers more than five hundred hectares, which is bigger than many city parks. It has become home to deer, rabbits, monkeys, birds, and even tigers and a group of elephants that visit every year. The trees also help to protect the island from the floods.

For many years, nobody outside the area knew about Jadav. Then, in two thousand seven, a journalist discovered the forest by accident and wrote about it. Jadav became famous, and people started calling him "the Forest Man of India." He has received many awards, but he still lives a simple life near his forest.

His story shows that one person, working patiently every day, can make an enormous difference. When people ask him why he did it, he simply says that nature gives us everything, so we should give something back.

=== b1-the-art-of-saying-no | The Art of Saying No | Psychology | B1
Do you find it difficult to say no? Maybe a colleague asks you to help with a project when you are already busy, or a friend wants you to come to a party when you really need to rest. Many people say yes because they don't want to disappoint others, and then they feel stressed and unhappy.

Psychologists say that this is very common. We are social animals, and we want people to like us. Saying no can feel rude or selfish, especially in some cultures where it is important to be polite and helpful. But if you always say yes, you will have no time or energy for the things that really matter to you.

The good news is that saying no is a skill, and you can learn it. Experts suggest starting with small things. You don't have to give a long explanation. A short, friendly answer is usually enough, for example: "Thank you for asking, but I can't this time." It is also useful to give yourself time to think. Instead of answering immediately, you can say, "Let me check my calendar and get back to you."

Another good strategy is to offer an alternative. If a colleague asks for help, you could say, "I can't do it today, but I could help you on Thursday morning." This shows that you care, but that your time is also important.

Learning to say no won't make people like you less. In fact, many people will respect you more. And when you do say yes, it will really mean something.

=== b1-the-night-i-got-stuck-in-the-elevator | Stuck in the Elevator | Short Fiction | B1
It was nearly midnight when Rachel finally left the office. She had been working on a report all evening, and she was exhausted. The building was empty and silent as she got into the elevator on the fourteenth floor and pressed the button for the ground floor.

The elevator had just passed the ninth floor when it suddenly stopped with a loud bang. The lights went off, and a moment later a small emergency light came on. Rachel pressed the alarm button, but nothing happened. She took out her phone. There was no signal.

At first, she tried to stay calm. She told herself that somebody would come soon. But after half an hour, she started to feel scared. It was getting hot, and she was thirsty. She sat on the floor and thought about her family, her cat, and all the things she had planned to do.

Then she heard a voice. "Hello? Is anybody in there?" It was the night security guard, an old man called Frank. He had noticed the elevator wasn't working on his computer screen. "Don't worry," he said through the doors. "The repair company is on its way. I'll stay here and talk to you until they come."

And he did. For the next hour, Frank told Rachel stories about his years as a sailor, and she found herself laughing. When the doors finally opened, she hugged him. The next day, she brought him a big box of chocolates. They have been friends ever since.

=== b1-why-we-need-sleep | Why Do We Need Sleep? | Science | B1
We spend about a third of our lives asleep. If you live to be ninety, that is around thirty years of sleeping. For a long time, scientists didn't really understand why we need so much sleep. Today, we know much more, and the answers are fascinating.

One of the most important jobs of sleep is to help our memory. During the day, our brain collects huge amounts of information. While we sleep, the brain sorts this information. It decides what is important, saves it in our long-term memory, and deletes what we don't need. That is why students who sleep well after studying usually remember more than students who stay up all night.

Sleep also cleans the brain. Scientists have discovered that while we sleep, a special liquid moves through the brain and washes away waste products. Some of these waste products are connected to diseases like Alzheimer's. Without enough sleep, they can build up over time.

The body uses sleep to repair itself too. During deep sleep, the body produces hormones that help muscles grow and heal. Sleep also helps our immune system to fight illnesses. People who regularly sleep less than six hours a night catch colds more often than people who sleep seven or eight hours.

So, if you want to stay healthy, sleep is not a waste of time. In fact, it may be one of the most important things you do every day. Next time you feel guilty about going to bed early, remember that your brain is doing important work.

=== b1-a-year-without-shopping | A Year Without Shopping | Society | B1
Two years ago, I made an unusual decision. I decided not to buy anything new for a whole year, except for food, medicine, and things for my hygiene. No new clothes, no new books, no new gadgets. My friends thought I wouldn't last a month.

I had several reasons. I was spending too much money, and I had a credit card debt that was making me anxious. My apartment was full of things I didn't use. I also felt guilty about the environment, because I knew that producing all these things uses a lot of energy and creates a lot of waste.

The first three months were the hardest. I didn't realize how often I bought things without thinking. When I was bored or sad, I used to go shopping online. Now I had to find other ways to feel better. I started going for walks, calling friends, and reading books from the library.

Something interesting happened. I started to appreciate the things I already had. I repaired a jacket instead of buying a new one, and I discovered that I really liked it. When I needed something, like a tent for a camping trip, I borrowed it from a neighbor. I also sold or gave away many of the things I didn't need.

At the end of the year, I had paid off my debt and saved a good amount of money. But the biggest change was in my mind. I learned that buying things doesn't make me happy for very long. Today I still shop, but much less, and I always ask myself first: do I really need this?

=== b1-the-history-of-chocolate | The Story of Chocolate | History | B1
Today, chocolate is one of the most popular sweets in the world. But for most of its history, it wasn't sweet at all. It was a bitter drink, and it was so valuable that people used it as money.

Chocolate comes from the seeds of the cacao tree, which grows in hot, wet places in Central and South America. More than three thousand years ago, people in this region started making a drink from the seeds. They roasted them, crushed them, and mixed them with water, chili, and spices. The Maya and the Aztecs believed the drink was a gift from the gods, and they used it in important ceremonies.

In the sixteenth century, Spanish explorers brought cacao back to Europe. At first, Europeans didn't like the bitter taste, but then somebody had the idea of adding sugar and honey. Hot chocolate became a fashionable drink for rich people, and chocolate houses opened in cities like London and Paris.

Solid chocolate, the kind we eat today, was only invented in the nineteenth century. New machines made it possible to produce chocolate cheaply, and soon chocolate bars were available to everybody. A Swiss inventor discovered how to add milk, and milk chocolate quickly became the most popular type.

Today, most of the world's cacao is grown in West Africa, not in Central America. Sadly, many cacao farmers are very poor, even though chocolate companies earn billions of dollars. That is why some people now choose to buy fair trade chocolate, which pays farmers a better price.

=== b1-the-power-of-walking | The Power of Walking | Health | B1
When people decide to get fit, they often think they need to join an expensive gym or start running long distances. But many doctors say that one of the best forms of exercise is also the simplest: walking.

Walking has a long list of benefits. Regular walking makes your heart and lungs stronger and helps to control your weight. It reduces the risk of many serious illnesses, such as heart disease and diabetes. It also strengthens your bones and muscles, which is especially important as you get older. And unlike running, it is gentle on your knees and back.

The benefits are not only physical. Research has shown that walking is very good for mental health. A thirty-minute walk can reduce stress and improve your mood. Walking in nature, such as in a park or a forest, seems to be even more effective. Many people also find that walking helps them think. Some famous writers and scientists used to go for long walks every day to find new ideas.

So how much should you walk? Many people try to walk ten thousand steps a day, which is about seven or eight kilometers. But recent studies suggest that even fewer steps, around seven thousand, can bring big health benefits. The most important thing is to walk regularly.

The best part is that walking is free, and you can do it almost anywhere. You can walk to work, take the stairs, or go for a walk after dinner with your family. It may be the easiest healthy habit you will ever start.

=== b1-a-new-life-abroad | Starting a New Life Abroad | Travel | B1
Five years ago, my husband got a job offer in Portugal. We had always dreamed of living abroad, so we decided to go. We sold our car, packed our lives into twelve boxes, and moved to Lisbon with our two young children. It was the most exciting and the most difficult thing we have ever done.

The first months were full of challenges. We didn't speak Portuguese, so even simple things like opening a bank account or going to the doctor were stressful. The paperwork seemed endless. I remember crying in a government office because I didn't understand what the woman was asking me. I felt lonely, and I missed my family and friends terribly.

The children adapted much faster than we did. Within six months, they were chatting in Portuguese with their new friends at school and correcting my pronunciation. Watching them gave me the motivation to try harder. I took evening classes and forced myself to speak, even though I made many mistakes.

Slowly, things got better. We made friends with our neighbors, who invited us to family dinners and taught us about local traditions. I found a job at an international school. We started to love the city: the bright light, the sea, the delicious food, and the relaxed way of life.

Today, Lisbon feels like home. Moving abroad taught us to be patient, flexible, and brave. It wasn't always easy, but I have never regretted our decision. If you ever get the chance, I would say: go for it.

=== b1-the-honest-robot | The Honest Robot | Technology | B1
In the near future, many homes may have robots that help with daily tasks. They could clean the house, cook dinner, and look after older people. But this raises an interesting question: should robots always tell the truth?

Imagine a robot that helps an old woman who lives alone. One day, she asks it, "Do I look nice in this dress?" The dress is old and doesn't fit her well. An honest robot would say no. A kind human might say yes, to make her happy. Which answer is better?

Scientists who design robots and artificial intelligence are thinking seriously about questions like this. Most people agree that robots should not lie about important things, such as medicine or safety. If a robot told an old man that he had taken his pills when he hadn't, the results could be dangerous. People also need to trust machines, and that trust would disappear quickly if robots lied.

However, human communication is complicated. People tell small lies every day to be polite or to protect other people's feelings. We say "I'm fine" when we are tired, or "What a lovely present!" when we don't really like it. A robot that is always completely honest might seem rude or cold.

Some researchers think robots should learn to be honest but kind, like a good friend. Instead of saying "That dress looks bad," a robot could say "I think the blue dress suits you even better." Teaching machines this kind of social skill is one of the biggest challenges in technology today.

=== b1-my-grandmothers-kitchen | My Grandmother's Kitchen | Family | B1
When I think about my childhood, the first thing I remember is my grandmother's kitchen. It was small and always warm, with a big wooden table in the middle and a window that looked out over her vegetable garden. There was always something cooking on the stove, and the room smelled of bread, onions, and herbs.

My grandmother never used recipes. She cooked everything from memory, using a little of this and a pinch of that. When I asked her how much flour to use, she would say, "Enough." When I asked how long to cook the soup, she would say, "Until it's ready." As a child, this drove me crazy. Now I understand that she cooked with her senses: she looked, smelled, and tasted all the time.

She taught me to cook when I was about eight. My first job was peeling potatoes, and I wasn't very good at it. Later, she showed me how to make bread, how to roll pasta, and how to tell when a cake was done by touching it gently. While we worked, she told me stories about her own childhood, during difficult times when food was scarce and nothing was ever wasted.

My grandmother died when I was twenty-two. A few years later, I tried to write down her recipes so I wouldn't forget them. It was impossible to get them exactly right. But I realized that her recipes were not really about the amounts. They were about patience, attention, and love.

Today, I cook for my own children in my own kitchen. And when they ask me how much salt to use, I hear myself say: "Enough."

=== b1-the-volunteer-firefighter | A Volunteer Firefighter | Work | B1
In many small towns and villages, there is no full-time fire department. Instead, the town depends on volunteer firefighters: ordinary people with normal jobs who are ready to help in an emergency at any time of the day or night. Tom Harris, a forty-five-year-old electrician, has been one of them for twenty years.

Tom carries a special pager with him everywhere he goes. When there is an emergency, it beeps loudly, and he has to leave whatever he is doing and drive to the fire station. "I've left in the middle of dinner, at my daughter's birthday party, and once even at the dentist," he laughs. "My family is used to it now."

The volunteers deal with much more than fires. They respond to car accidents, floods, and storms. They rescue people trapped in cars, help with medical emergencies, and sometimes even rescue animals. To do this, they need a lot of training. Tom spends two evenings a month practicing with the team, and he has taken courses in first aid and rescue techniques.

The job can be frightening and emotionally difficult. Tom has seen terrible accidents, and some nights he can't sleep afterward. But he says the team supports each other, and they often talk about difficult experiences together.

Why does he do it, without being paid? "This is my community," he says simply. "These are my neighbors. If my house was on fire, I'd want somebody to come. So I think I should be that somebody for other people."

=== b1-the-mystery-of-the-moai | The Statues of Easter Island | History | B1
Easter Island is one of the most isolated places on Earth. It is a small island in the Pacific Ocean, more than three thousand five hundred kilometers from the coast of Chile. But it is famous all over the world because of its huge stone statues, called moai.

There are almost a thousand moai on the island. Most of them are about four meters tall, but the largest one is almost ten meters tall and weighs more than eighty tons. They were carved from volcanic rock between about seven hundred and five hundred years ago by the Rapa Nui people, who had arrived on the island by canoe many centuries earlier.

For a long time, the statues were a mystery. How did people without machines, metal tools, or large animals move such heavy objects across the island? Some people suggested strange theories, even saying that aliens had helped. But scientists have found a simpler answer. Experiments have shown that a small group of people with ropes could "walk" a statue by rocking it from side to side. Local legends say that the moai walked to their places, so the old stories may have been true in a way.

Another mystery is what happened to the island's society. When Europeans arrived in the eighteenth century, the forests had disappeared, and the population was much smaller. Some scientists blame the islanders for cutting down too many trees. Others believe that rats and diseases brought by Europeans caused most of the damage.

Today, the moai are protected as a World Heritage Site, and they remind us of the skill and determination of the people who made them.

=== b1-fake-news | How to Spot Fake News | Media | B1
Every day, we see hundreds of news stories on our phones and computers. Some of them are true, but some are false or misleading. These are often called "fake news." They can spread very quickly on social media, sometimes faster than real news, because they are often shocking or emotional.

Why do people create fake news? Some do it to make money, because shocking stories get a lot of clicks, and clicks bring money from advertising. Others do it for political reasons, to make people angry or to influence their opinions. And sometimes, people simply share stories without checking them, because they believe them or because they think they are funny.

So how can you tell if a story is true? First, look at the source. Is it a well-known news organization, or a website you have never heard of? Check the address of the website carefully, because some fake sites copy the names of real ones. Second, read more than the headline. Headlines are often exaggerated, and the real story may be quite different.

Third, check the date. Sometimes old stories are shared again as if they were new. Fourth, search for the story on other websites. If something important has really happened, many news organizations will be reporting it. Finally, be careful with photos. Pictures can be edited or taken from a completely different event.

The most important thing is to stop and think before you share. If a story makes you very angry or very excited, that is a good moment to check it. We all have a responsibility to stop false information from spreading.

=== b1-the-street-musician | The Violinist in the Subway | Culture | B1
On a cold morning in January two thousand seven, a young man stood in a subway station in Washington and played the violin. It was rush hour, and over the next forty-five minutes, more than a thousand people walked past him on their way to work.

Very few of them stopped. A few threw some coins into his violin case without looking at him. A small boy tried to stop and listen, but his mother pulled him away. In total, the musician earned about thirty-two dollars. When he finished, nobody clapped.

What the commuters didn't know was that the violinist was Joshua Bell, one of the most famous classical musicians in the world. Just a few days earlier, he had played to a full concert hall where tickets cost about a hundred dollars each. And his violin was worth several million dollars.

The event was an experiment organized by a newspaper. The journalists wanted to find out whether people would recognize beauty in an unexpected place, when they were busy and not expecting it. The answer, it seemed, was mostly no.

The story became very famous, and people still discuss what it means. Some say it shows that modern life is too busy, and that we don't notice the beautiful things around us. Others say it is not fair to judge the commuters, because they had to get to work on time. Interestingly, the people who were most likely to stop were children. Perhaps we can learn something from them.

=== b1-the-gap-year | Taking a Gap Year | Education | B1
In some countries, it is quite common for young people to take a "gap year" between finishing school and starting university. During this year, they might travel, work, volunteer, or learn new skills. For many, it is a chance to discover more about the world and about themselves.

Supporters of gap years say they have many benefits. After years of exams and studying, students often feel tired and unsure about what they really want to do. A year away from education gives them time to rest and to think about their future. Many come back more mature and more motivated. Some even change their plans completely after discovering a new interest.

Traveling and working abroad can also help young people become more independent. They learn to manage money, solve problems on their own, and communicate with people from different cultures. These are skills that employers value highly. Volunteering can give them practical experience and a better understanding of social problems.

However, gap years also have disadvantages. Traveling can be expensive, and not every family can afford it. Some students worry that they will forget what they learned at school, or that they will lose the habit of studying. There is also a risk that a gap year will become a wasted year, if a student doesn't plan it carefully and spends most of the time doing nothing.

In the end, a gap year is a personal decision. Experts say the most important thing is to have a clear plan and a purpose. A well-planned gap year can be one of the most valuable experiences of a young person's life.

=== b1-the-last-letter | The Last Letter | Short Fiction | B1
When Helen's father died, she had to empty his house. It was a small house in the countryside where he had lived alone for many years since her mother's death. Helen hadn't visited very often. They had argued a lot when she was young, and they had never really been close.

On the third day, she was clearing out his desk when she found a wooden box at the back of a drawer. Inside, there were dozens of letters, all addressed to her. None of them had been sent. The first was dated twenty years ago, the week she had left home after a terrible argument.

Helen sat on the floor and began to read. In the letters, her father wrote about his life: the garden, the weather, his loneliness after her mother died. But mostly, he wrote about her. He wrote about how proud he was when he heard she had become a doctor. He wrote about how much he regretted the angry words he had said. In every letter, he asked her to forgive him.

The last letter had been written only a month before he died. "I know I am not good at saying these things," it said. "I have tried to send these letters many times, but I was always afraid. I hope one day you will read them and know that I always loved you."

Helen read the letters until it was dark. Then she went into the garden and sat under the apple tree that her father had planted when she was born. For the first time in years, she cried for him.

=== b1-the-invention-of-the-bicycle | The Bicycle | Technology | B1
Today, there are more than a billion bicycles in the world, about twice as many as cars. They are cheap, healthy, and good for the environment. But the bicycle we know today took a long time to develop.

The first machine that looked a little like a bicycle was invented in Germany in eighteen seventeen. It was made of wood and had two wheels, but no pedals. To move, the rider pushed against the ground with their feet, like a child on a balance bike. It was called a "running machine," and for a short time it was quite fashionable.

About fifty years later, inventors in France added pedals to the front wheel. These bicycles were very uncomfortable, because the wheels were made of wood and iron. People called them "boneshakers." Later came the famous "penny-farthing," with a huge front wheel and a tiny back wheel. It was fast, but very dangerous, because riders sat high above the ground and often fell off.

The modern bicycle appeared in the eighteen eighties. It had two wheels of the same size, a chain connected to the back wheel, and, soon after, air-filled rubber tires. It was safe, comfortable, and easy to ride. Suddenly, bicycles became very popular, especially with women, because they gave them a new kind of freedom.

Today, many cities are building bike lanes and encouraging people to cycle instead of drive. After two hundred years, the simple bicycle may be more important than ever.

=== b1-learning-from-failure | Learning from Failure | Psychology | B1
Nobody likes to fail. When we fail an exam, lose a job, or make a mistake at work, we often feel embarrassed and disappointed. Many of us are afraid of failure, so we avoid taking risks. But some psychologists believe that failure is one of the best ways to learn.

Think about how a baby learns to walk. It falls down hundreds of times, but it doesn't give up. Each fall teaches it something about balance. Adults, however, often stop trying after one or two failures, because they worry about what other people think.

Many successful people have experienced big failures. The writer J. K. Rowling was rejected by twelve publishers before her first Harry Potter book was accepted. The inventor Thomas Edison made thousands of attempts before he created a light bulb that worked. When people asked him about his failures, he reportedly said, "I have not failed. I've just found ten thousand ways that won't work."

Psychologists talk about a "growth mindset." People with a growth mindset believe that their abilities can improve with effort and practice. When they fail, they ask, "What can I learn from this?" People with a "fixed mindset" believe their abilities cannot change, so failure feels like proof that they are not good enough.

The good news is that we can develop a growth mindset. One simple way is to change the way we talk to ourselves. Instead of saying "I'm bad at this," we can say "I'm not good at this yet." That small word, "yet," can make a big difference.

=== b1-the-city-of-canals | Living in Amsterdam | Places | B1
Amsterdam, the capital of the Netherlands, is famous for its canals, bicycles, and narrow old houses. I moved there for work three years ago, and I still feel like a tourist sometimes when I walk along the water on a sunny evening.

The city center was built in the seventeenth century, when Amsterdam was one of the richest cities in the world. Merchants built tall, thin houses along the canals, because taxes were based on the width of the house. Many houses lean forward slightly, and they have hooks at the top. These were used to lift furniture up and in through the windows, because the stairs inside are very steep and narrow.

The most surprising thing for me was the bicycles. There are more bikes than people in Amsterdam, and everybody cycles, from businesspeople in suits to parents with three children in a box at the front. At first, I was terrified of the busy bike lanes, but now I can't imagine life without my bike. It is the fastest way to get around.

Life in Amsterdam is not perfect. Housing is very expensive, and it can be hard to find an apartment. The weather is often gray and wet, and the wind can be very strong. In summer, the city center is crowded with tourists.

But I love the relaxed atmosphere, the beautiful architecture, and the way people enjoy life. On summer weekends, people sit on their boats or by the canals with friends, and the whole city seems to be having a picnic.
