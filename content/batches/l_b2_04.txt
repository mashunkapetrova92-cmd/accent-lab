=== b2-the-science-of-motivation | What Really Motivates Us? | Psychology | B2
For much of the twentieth century, many businesses assumed that the best way to motivate people was through rewards and punishments. If you wanted employees to work harder, you offered them bonuses. If you wanted them to avoid mistakes, you threatened them with consequences. This approach, sometimes called the "carrot and stick," seems logical. Yet decades of research in psychology suggest that human motivation is considerably more complicated.

Psychologists distinguish between two main types of motivation. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside rewards, such as money, grades, or praise. Intrinsic motivation comes from within: we do something because we find it interesting, enjoyable, or meaningful. A child who reads books for pleasure is intrinsically motivated, while a child who reads only to earn stickers is extrinsically motivated.

Surprisingly, research has found that extrinsic rewards can sometimes reduce intrinsic motivation. In a classic experiment, children who enjoyed drawing were divided into groups. Some were promised a certificate for drawing, while others were not. Later, when given free time, the children who had received rewards spent less time drawing than the others. Drawing had become work rather than play. Psychologists call this the overjustification effect.

Rewards are not always harmful. For simple, routine tasks, financial incentives can increase effort and performance. But for complex tasks that require creativity and problem-solving, large rewards can sometimes make people perform worse, perhaps because the pressure narrows their focus.

According to self-determination theory, developed by psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, people are most motivated when three basic psychological needs are satisfied. The first is autonomy, the feeling that we have control over our actions. The second is competence, the sense that we are good at what we do and improving. The third is relatedness, the feeling of being connected to other people. When these needs are met, people tend to be more engaged, more creative, and more satisfied.

These findings have important implications for schools, workplaces, and parenting. Giving people choices, offering opportunities to develop their skills, providing helpful feedback, and building supportive relationships may be more effective than relying solely on rewards and punishments. Some companies have experimented with giving employees time to work on projects of their own choosing, and several successful products have emerged from such initiatives. Motivation, it seems, cannot simply be bought.

=== b2-the-ghost-towns-of-japan | Japan's Empty Houses | Society | B2
In many parts of the world, finding an affordable home is a major challenge. In Japan, however, a very different problem has emerged. Across the country, there are millions of empty houses, known as "akiya." Some estimates suggest that around one in seven homes in Japan is now unoccupied, and the number is rising.

The main cause is demographic change. Japan has one of the oldest populations in the world, and its population has been shrinking for over a decade. The birth rate is low, and relatively few immigrants settle in the country. Meanwhile, young people have been moving from rural areas to large cities such as Tokyo and Osaka in search of jobs and opportunities. As older residents in the countryside die or move into care homes, their houses are often left empty.

Several cultural and economic factors make the problem worse. In Japan, houses have traditionally been seen as losing value over time, unlike in many other countries. Many buyers prefer new homes, and older houses are often considered to be worth very little after a few decades. Inheriting an old house in the countryside can be more of a burden than a gift, because the owners must pay taxes and maintenance costs. Demolishing a house can be expensive too, and in some cases, owners simply cannot be found.

Empty houses can cause problems for local communities. They may become dangerous as they deteriorate, especially in a country where earthquakes and typhoons are common. They can attract pests, and they contribute to a sense of decline in villages that are already losing their residents.

In response, many local governments have created "akiya banks," online databases that list empty houses for sale or rent, sometimes at extremely low prices or even for free. Some towns offer financial support to people who move there and renovate an old house, particularly young families. A number of foreigners have taken advantage of these opportunities, buying traditional homes and turning them into guesthouses, cafes, or artists' studios.

Some see the empty houses as an opportunity to revitalize rural Japan, attracting remote workers, entrepreneurs, and people seeking a quieter life. However, experts caution that without jobs, schools, and services, it will be difficult to reverse the decline of many rural communities. The akiya problem reflects a challenge that many countries with aging populations may face in the future.

=== b2-the-woman-who-walked-the-world | Walking Away from Everything | Travel | B2
In nineteen eighty-three, a sixteen-year-old British girl named Ffyona Campbell set out from the northern tip of Scotland to walk the length of Britain. It was the beginning of an extraordinary journey that would last eleven years and take her across four continents. By the time she finished, she had walked around thirty-two thousand kilometers and become known as the first woman to walk around the world.

Campbell's childhood had been unsettled. Her father was an officer in the navy, and the family moved frequently, so she attended many different schools and found it hard to make lasting friendships. She later said that walking gave her a sense of belonging and purpose that she had never felt before.

After Britain, she walked across the United States, from New York to Los Angeles. She then walked across Australia, from Sydney to Perth, completing the five thousand kilometer journey in record time. The most challenging stage was Africa, which she crossed from Cape Town in the south to Tangier in the north. The journey took two years and involved walking through deserts, jungles, and areas affected by conflict. She faced extreme heat, illness, and the constant danger of wild animals. Along the way, she was helped by local people, who offered her food, water, and shelter.

Finally, she walked across Europe, from Spain back to Britain, and returned to the place where she had started. Her achievement brought her international fame and raised money for charity.

However, Campbell's story took a surprising turn. In nineteen ninety-six, she publicly admitted that during her walk across the United States, she had accepted rides in a support vehicle for part of the distance, because she was exhausted and unwell. She said that the guilt had haunted her for years and that telling the truth was the only way to free herself from it.

Her honesty divided opinion. Some people criticized her for having claimed a record she had not fully earned. Others admired her for confessing and correcting her mistake. In later years, Campbell spoke openly about the emotional difficulties she had experienced and about what walking had taught her about herself. Her story raises interesting questions about truth, pressure, and the meaning of achievement.

=== b2-the-curse-of-knowledge | Why Experts Are Bad Teachers | Psychology | B2
Have you ever asked an expert for help and received an explanation that was impossible to understand? Perhaps a computer technician used terms you had never heard of, or a teacher skipped steps that seemed obvious to them but were confusing to you. This common problem has a name. Psychologists call it the curse of knowledge.

The curse of knowledge occurs when someone who knows a lot about a topic finds it difficult to imagine what it is like not to know it. Once we have learned something, it becomes very hard to remember how we thought about it before. As a result, experts often overestimate how much other people know, use specialist language without explaining it, and leave out information that seems obvious to them.

One famous demonstration of the effect was an experiment carried out at Stanford University in the nineteen nineties. Participants were divided into two groups: tappers and listeners. The tappers were asked to choose a well-known song, such as "Happy Birthday," and tap out its rhythm on a table. The listeners had to guess the song. Before the experiment, the tappers predicted that listeners would correctly identify about half of the songs. In reality, listeners guessed correctly only about one time in forty.

The reason for the difference was simple. When the tappers tapped, they could hear the melody in their heads. To them, the song seemed obvious. But the listeners heard only a series of knocks on a table. The tappers simply could not imagine what it was like to hear the rhythm without the melody.

The curse of knowledge affects many areas of life. It can make instruction manuals confusing, business presentations unclear, and scientific research difficult for the public to understand. It can also cause misunderstandings in workplaces, when managers assume that employees know things they have never been told.

Fortunately, there are ways to reduce its effects. Experts can test their explanations on people who know little about the subject and ask for honest feedback. Using concrete examples, stories, and comparisons with familiar things can make abstract ideas easier to understand. Breaking information into small steps and avoiding unnecessary jargon also helps. Perhaps most importantly, experts can try to remember that what seems obvious to them was once a mystery, and that good teaching begins with understanding the learner's point of view.

=== b2-the-frozen-man | Otzi the Iceman | History | B2
In September nineteen ninety-one, two German hikers walking in the Alps near the border between Austria and Italy noticed something strange in a melting glacier. Sticking out of the ice was the upper body of a man. At first, they assumed he was a mountaineer who had died in an accident, perhaps a few decades earlier. They were wrong by more than five thousand years.

The man, who became known as Otzi, after the Otztal valley where he was found, had lived around three thousand three hundred years before Christ. His body had been preserved naturally by the ice, making him one of the oldest and best-preserved human bodies ever discovered. Scientists were amazed. For the first time, they could study in detail a person from the Copper Age, along with his clothes and equipment.

Otzi was about one meter sixty tall and around forty-five years old when he died. He wore clothes made from the skins of several different animals, including a coat, leggings, a bearskin hat, and shoes stuffed with grass for warmth. He carried a copper axe, a flint knife, a bow and arrows, and a small bag containing tools and materials for making fire. Remarkably, his body was covered in more than sixty tattoos, mostly simple lines and crosses, located on joints that may have been painful. Some researchers believe the tattoos were a form of medical treatment.

Scientists analyzed the contents of his stomach and discovered that his last meal included wild goat meat and a type of ancient wheat. Pollen found in his body allowed them to work out that he had died in spring or early summer.

For years, it was assumed that Otzi had died from cold and exhaustion. However, in two thousand one, an X-ray revealed something surprising: an arrowhead lodged in his left shoulder. The arrow had damaged an important artery, and he probably died within minutes. Further examination showed that he had also suffered a blow to the head. Otzi had been murdered.

Who killed him, and why, remains a mystery. Some researchers believe he was involved in a conflict with another group, while others suspect a personal dispute. Today, Otzi lies in a specially cooled room at a museum in Bolzano, Italy, where visitors can see him through a small window. More than thirty years after his discovery, he continues to reveal secrets about life in prehistoric Europe.

=== b2-the-hidden-world-of-fungi | The Kingdom of Fungi | Nature | B2
When most people think of fungi, they picture mushrooms in a forest or mold on an old piece of bread. Yet these visible forms are only a tiny part of an enormous and largely hidden kingdom of life. Scientists estimate that there may be several million species of fungi on Earth, of which only a small fraction have been identified and described.

Fungi are neither plants nor animals. In fact, genetic studies have shown that they are more closely related to animals than to plants. Unlike plants, they cannot produce their own food through photosynthesis. Instead, they obtain nutrients by breaking down organic material or by forming partnerships with other organisms.

Most of the body of a fungus is made up of a network of extremely thin threads called hyphae, which spread through soil, wood, and other materials. Together, these threads form a structure called a mycelium. What we call a mushroom is simply the reproductive part of the fungus, which produces spores, much as an apple is the fruit of an apple tree. Some fungal networks can be enormous. In a forest in the American state of Oregon, a single fungus covers an area of nearly ten square kilometers and is believed to be thousands of years old, making it one of the largest living organisms on the planet.

Fungi play essential roles in nature. As decomposers, they break down dead leaves, wood, and animals, recycling nutrients back into the soil. Without them, forests would be buried under piles of dead material. Many fungi also form partnerships with the roots of plants, helping them to absorb water and nutrients in exchange for sugars. The majority of plant species depend on such relationships.

Fungi have also transformed human life. Yeast, a type of fungus, is used to make bread, beer, and wine. Penicillin, the first antibiotic, was discovered in a mold. Other fungi are used to produce medicines, food ingredients, and industrial enzymes.

Researchers are now exploring new uses for fungi. Some companies are developing materials made from mycelium that can replace plastic packaging, leather, and even building materials. Scientists are also investigating whether certain fungi can help to break down pollutants, such as oil and plastics. However, fungi can also cause serious diseases in plants, animals, and humans, and some are becoming resistant to treatments. Understanding this hidden kingdom has never been more important.

=== b2-the-tutor | The Boy Who Couldn't Read | Short Fiction | B2
Emma had been volunteering at the community center for only two weeks when the coordinator introduced her to Leo. He was eleven years old, small for his age, and he sat with his arms crossed, staring at the table. "Leo's reading is a little behind," the coordinator said quietly. Later, Emma discovered that this was a polite understatement. Leo could barely read at all.

For the first three sessions, he refused to try. He pushed the books away, made jokes, and asked to go to the bathroom every ten minutes. Emma, who was a university student studying literature, began to wonder if she was the wrong person for this. Then, during the fourth session, she noticed that while she was talking, Leo was drawing in the margins of his notebook. They were not childish doodles. They were detailed, skillful drawings of superheroes, dragons, and spaceships.

"These are amazing," she said. Leo shrugged, but she saw a flicker of pride in his eyes. She had an idea. "What if we made a comic together? You draw it, and we write the words." He looked at her suspiciously. "I can't write," he said. "Then I'll help you," she replied.

Over the following months, they worked on a comic about a boy who could turn invisible. Leo drew every page, and Emma helped him plan the story. At first, she wrote the words while he dictated, and then she encouraged him to copy them into the speech bubbles himself. Slowly, he began to recognize words, then to sound them out, then to read short sentences on his own. He wanted to know what his characters were saying, and that desire made him persevere when the letters seemed to dance on the page.

Emma later learned that Leo had dyslexia, which had never been properly diagnosed. Partly as a result of her concerns, the school arranged an assessment and additional support for him.

By the end of the school year, the comic was finished: thirty pages, carefully colored, with a cover that read "The Invisible Boy, by Leo Martin." The community center printed a few copies, and Leo read it aloud at a small celebration, stumbling over some words but refusing to stop. When he finished, everybody clapped. He looked at Emma and grinned. "Next one," he said, "is going to be about a dragon."

=== b2-the-race-to-the-south-pole | The Race to the South Pole | History | B2
In the early twentieth century, the South Pole was one of the last great unexplored places on Earth. Several countries competed to be the first to reach it, and in nineteen eleven, two expeditions set out on what became one of the most famous races in history. One was led by the Norwegian explorer Roald Amundsen. The other was led by a British naval officer, Captain Robert Falcon Scott.

The two teams took very different approaches. Amundsen had spent time living with Indigenous peoples in the Arctic and had learned from them how to survive in extreme cold. His team wore loose fur clothing and relied on dogs to pull their sleds. They were also expert skiers. Amundsen's plan was simple and focused entirely on reaching the Pole quickly and returning safely.

Scott's expedition was larger and had broader scientific goals. Instead of relying mainly on dogs, Scott chose to use a combination of motor sledges, ponies, and men pulling sleds themselves. The motor sledges broke down early, and the ponies, unsuited to the extreme conditions, struggled and eventually had to be shot. For much of the journey, Scott's men pulled their heavy sleds on foot, an exhausting task in temperatures far below freezing.

Amundsen's team reached the South Pole on the fourteenth of December, nineteen eleven. They left a tent, a Norwegian flag, and a letter for Scott, and then returned safely to their base. More than a month later, on the seventeenth of January, nineteen twelve, Scott and four companions finally reached the Pole, only to find the Norwegian flag waiting for them. Scott wrote in his diary: "The worst has happened. All the day dreams must go."

The return journey ended in tragedy. Exhausted, hungry, and suffering from frostbite, Scott's team struggled through terrible weather. One by one, the men died. Scott and his last two companions died in their tent, just eighteen kilometers from a supply depot that might have saved them. Their bodies, along with Scott's diaries, were found eight months later.

In Britain, Scott was celebrated for many years as a tragic hero. In more recent decades, historians have debated his decisions, with some blaming poor planning and others pointing to exceptionally bad weather. Amundsen's success, meanwhile, is often attributed to careful preparation and his willingness to learn from people who knew the environment best.

=== b2-the-smartest-bird | The Clever Crows | Animals | B2
For a long time, people assumed that intelligence was something that only large-brained animals, such as humans, apes, and dolphins, possessed. Birds, with their small heads, were often considered simple creatures. The insult "birdbrain" reflects this belief. However, research over the past few decades has revealed that some birds, particularly members of the crow family, are astonishingly intelligent.

Crows, ravens, and their relatives have demonstrated abilities that rival those of great apes. New Caledonian crows, which live on a group of islands in the Pacific Ocean, are famous for making and using tools. In the wild, they shape twigs and leaves into hooks to extract insects from holes in trees. In laboratory experiments, they have solved complex puzzles that require several steps, using one tool to obtain another, which they then use to reach food.

In one remarkable experiment, crows were presented with a tube of water containing a floating treat that was too low to reach. The crows dropped stones into the water to raise its level until they could grab the treat, much like the crow in one of Aesop's ancient fables. They also learned to choose heavy objects that sank rather than light ones that floated.

Crows also seem to have excellent memories, especially for faces. In a study in the United States, researchers wearing a particular mask captured and released some wild crows. For years afterward, the crows scolded and dive-bombed anyone wearing that mask, even if they had not been captured themselves. They appeared to have learned from other crows that the mask represented danger, and passed this knowledge on to younger birds.

Ravens have shown evidence of planning for the future, a skill once thought to be uniquely human. In experiments, they selected and saved tools that they would need later to open a box containing food, even when there was a delay of many hours.

How can birds with such small brains be so clever? Scientists have discovered that bird brains are packed with neurons, much more densely than mammal brains. Some birds have as many neurons in their brains as certain monkeys. These discoveries have forced scientists to rethink what intelligence is and how it evolves. They also remind us that intelligence can take many forms, and that we may still be underestimating the minds of the animals around us.

=== b2-the-power-of-storytelling | Why Our Brains Love Stories | Culture | B2
Long before writing was invented, humans told stories. Around campfires, in caves, and in villages, people shared tales of heroes, monsters, gods, and ancestors. Every known culture has a tradition of storytelling, and today, we consume stories constantly through books, films, television series, video games, and even advertisements. Why do stories have such a powerful hold on us?

One explanation is that stories helped our ancestors survive. Before written language, stories were a way of passing on important knowledge, such as where to find food, which plants were dangerous, and how to behave within a group. A story about a person who wandered too far from the village and was attacked by a wild animal was more memorable than a simple warning. Stories also helped to strengthen social bonds and shared values.

Research in psychology and neuroscience has shown that stories affect our brains in unique ways. When we hear a list of facts, only the language areas of our brain are activated. But when we listen to a story, many other areas become active as well, including those involved in movement, emotion, and the senses. If a character in a story runs, the parts of our brain that control running may become more active. In a sense, we experience the story as if it were happening to us.

Stories can also influence our emotions and behavior. In one study, participants watched a short, emotional story about a father whose young son was seriously ill. Afterward, their brains showed increased levels of oxytocin, a hormone associated with trust and empathy. Participants with higher levels of this hormone were more willing to donate money to charity.

This power has not gone unnoticed by businesses, politicians, and campaigners. Advertisers frequently use stories to sell products, and charities often focus on the experience of a single individual rather than presenting statistics. Research suggests that people tend to donate more when they hear about one specific person in need than when they are told about thousands of people.

However, the persuasive power of stories can also be misused. Misleading or false stories can spread quickly and influence people's beliefs more effectively than accurate data. For this reason, some experts argue that learning to think critically about the stories we hear is an essential skill. Stories can inform and inspire us, but they can also deceive.

=== b2-the-floating-schools | Schools on the Water | Education | B2
In northern Bangladesh, the monsoon season brings heavy rains that flood large areas of land every year. For several months, roads disappear under water, villages become islands, and many schools are forced to close. For children living in these regions, this often means missing a large part of their education. In the early two thousands, an architect named Mohammed Rezwan came up with an innovative solution: if children could not get to school, the school would come to them.

Rezwan grew up in the region and had experienced the problem himself. As a child, he sometimes could not attend school because of floods. After studying architecture, he founded a nonprofit organization and began converting traditional wooden boats into floating schools. Each boat has a classroom that can hold around thirty students, with a roof covered in solar panels that provide electricity for lights, computers, and other equipment.

The boats travel along the rivers, stopping at different villages to collect children, much like a school bus. Lessons are held on board, either while the boat is moving or when it is anchored near the riverbank. Afterward, the boat takes the children home and collects another group.

The organization has expanded the idea in creative ways. In addition to schools, it has created floating libraries, floating health clinics, and floating training centers where adults, particularly women, can learn skills such as farming and handicrafts. Some boats even teach farmers how to grow food on floating gardens, which can rise and fall with the water level.

The project has received international recognition and has been copied in other countries that face similar challenges. It is often cited as an example of how communities can adapt to climate change, which is expected to make flooding more frequent and severe in many parts of the world. Bangladesh, a low-lying country with many rivers, is considered one of the nations most vulnerable to rising sea levels.

Rezwan has often said that the most important lesson of the project is to work with nature rather than against it. Instead of building expensive barriers to keep water out, the floating schools accept the reality of floods and find ways to continue learning despite them. For thousands of children, the boats have turned the river, once an obstacle to education, into a path toward it.

=== b2-the-psychology-of-queues | Why We Hate Waiting | Psychology | B2
Most of us spend a surprising amount of our lives waiting: in lines at supermarkets, at airports, in traffic, on the phone with customer service, or for a web page to load. Some estimates suggest that the average person spends many months of their life waiting in lines. Waiting is rarely enjoyable, but research suggests that how we feel about it depends less on how long we actually wait than on how the wait is experienced.

One of the pioneers of research into the psychology of waiting was David Maister, who proposed a number of principles in the nineteen eighties. One of these is that unoccupied time feels longer than occupied time. This is why many businesses try to keep us busy while we wait. Restaurants hand out menus to people waiting for tables, and elevators often have mirrors beside them. According to one well-known story, a hotel received many complaints about slow elevators. Instead of installing faster elevators, which would have been very expensive, the managers placed mirrors next to them. The complaints almost stopped, because people were busy looking at themselves.

Another principle is that uncertain waits feel longer than known, finite waits. When we do not know how long we will have to wait, we become anxious and impatient. This is why many transportation systems now display the time until the next train or bus, and why theme parks show estimated waiting times for rides. Interestingly, theme parks often overestimate the wait slightly, so visitors are pleasantly surprised when they reach the front sooner than expected.

Fairness is also crucial. People become extremely frustrated when they feel that others are being served out of turn. A single line serving several counters is often perceived as fairer than separate lines for each counter, even if the average waiting time is the same, because nobody has to worry about choosing the wrong line.

The end of an experience also strongly influences how we remember it. If a long wait is followed by excellent service, people often remember the experience positively. Conversely, a short wait followed by rude service may leave a bad impression.

These insights are used by businesses around the world to manage the experience of waiting. They also offer lessons for individuals. Bringing a book, focusing on something interesting, or simply accepting the wait rather than fighting it can make the time pass more pleasantly.

=== b2-the-chocolate-factory | The Sweet Problem of Child Labor | Business | B2
Chocolate is one of the world's favorite treats, enjoyed by people of all ages. The global chocolate industry is worth well over a hundred billion dollars a year. Yet behind the colorful wrappers lies a troubling reality. A large share of the world's cocoa, the main ingredient in chocolate, is produced in West Africa, particularly in Ivory Coast and Ghana, and research has found that more than a million children work on cocoa farms in these two countries, often in dangerous conditions.

Cocoa is mostly grown on small family farms, typically only a few hectares in size. Many cocoa farmers live in extreme poverty, earning far less than is needed to support their families. Because they cannot afford to hire adult workers, they often rely on their own children, or sometimes on children from other families, to help with the harvest.

The work can be hazardous. Children may use sharp machetes to cut open cocoa pods, carry heavy loads, and handle pesticides without protective equipment. Many miss school as a result. In the most serious cases, children have been trafficked from neighboring countries and forced to work without pay.

The problem received international attention in the early two thousands, and in two thousand one, major chocolate companies signed an agreement promising to eliminate the worst forms of child labor in their supply chains. However, the deadline was extended several times, and studies have shown that the number of children working in hazardous conditions has not significantly decreased.

Many experts argue that the root cause of the problem is poverty, and that it cannot be solved without ensuring that farmers receive a fair price for their cocoa. The price farmers receive is often only a small fraction of the price of a chocolate bar. Some initiatives aim to address this, including fair trade certification and programs that pay farmers a premium for meeting certain standards. Some companies have begun tracing their cocoa back to individual farms and investing in local schools and community programs.

The governments of Ivory Coast and Ghana have also introduced a minimum price policy designed to increase farmers' incomes. Nevertheless, progress has been slow. For consumers, choosing chocolate from companies that are transparent about their supply chains and pay fairer prices can make a small difference. Ultimately, however, many campaigners believe that lasting change will require stronger laws and greater responsibility from the companies that profit from cocoa.

=== b2-the-art-of-doing-nothing | The Italian Art of Doing Nothing | Culture | B2
In a world that celebrates productivity, being busy has become a kind of status symbol. Many people fill their days with work, exercise, social engagements, and self-improvement, and they feel guilty when they are not doing something useful. In Italy, however, there is an expression that captures a very different attitude: "il dolce far niente," which means "the sweetness of doing nothing."

The phrase describes the pleasure of being idle, of relaxing without guilt and simply enjoying the moment. It might mean sitting in a sunny square watching people pass by, lingering over a long lunch with friends, or taking a slow walk through the streets in the evening. The point is not to accomplish anything, but to appreciate life as it is.

This attitude is reflected in several Italian customs. In many towns, especially in the south, people take part in the "passeggiata," a leisurely evening stroll through the main streets. Families, couples, and groups of friends dress up and walk slowly, stopping to chat with neighbors and acquaintances. It is a social ritual rather than a form of exercise, and its purpose is simply to enjoy the company of others and the pleasant evening air.

Other cultures have similar concepts. The Dutch have recently popularized "niksen," which literally means "doing nothing," as a way to reduce stress. In Denmark, the idea of "hygge" describes a cozy feeling of contentment and comfort, often enjoyed at home with friends, candles, and good food.

Research suggests that periods of rest and idleness can have real benefits. When we stop focusing on tasks, our brains do not simply switch off. Instead, they enter a different mode of activity, which is associated with creativity, reflection, and the processing of memories and emotions. Taking regular breaks can also reduce stress and prevent burnout.

Of course, doing nothing is not always easy. Many people feel restless or anxious when they are not busy, and the constant availability of smartphones makes it tempting to fill every spare moment. Learning to do nothing may require practice. Experts suggest starting small, perhaps by sitting quietly for a few minutes without a phone, looking out of a window, or taking a walk without a destination. As the Italians might say, life is not only about doing, but also about being.

=== b2-the-unlikely-inventor | The Woman Who Invented the Windshield Wiper | Technology | B2
In nineteen oh two, a woman named Mary Anderson was visiting New York City on a cold winter day. She was riding a streetcar through the snow when she noticed that the driver was having great difficulty seeing through the windshield. To clear the glass, he had to open the window, letting in the freezing air, or stop the vehicle and get out to wipe off the snow and ice by hand. Many drivers simply put their heads out of the side window.

Anderson thought there had to be a better solution. When she returned home to Alabama, she began sketching an idea. Her design consisted of a rubber blade attached to an arm, which could be operated from inside the vehicle using a handle. When the driver turned the handle, the blade would swing across the windshield, clearing away rain, snow, and sleet. When it was not needed, the device could be removed.

In nineteen oh three, Anderson was granted a patent for her invention. She then tried to sell the rights to a company in Canada, but they rejected it, saying that it had no commercial value. At the time, there were relatively few cars on the roads, and many people believed that a moving blade would distract drivers and cause accidents.

Anderson's patent expired in nineteen twenty, and she never made any money from her invention. Shortly afterward, as cars became more common, windshield wipers became standard equipment. Several other inventors developed improvements. One of the most important was an automatic wiper, patented in nineteen seventeen by another woman, Charlotte Bridgwood, which used electric rollers rather than a manual handle.

Today, windshield wipers are found on virtually every car, bus, and truck in the world, and they are an essential safety feature. Yet for many decades, Anderson's contribution was largely forgotten. Her story is one of many examples of inventors, particularly women, whose ideas were ahead of their time and who did not receive the recognition or financial reward they deserved.

In two thousand eleven, Mary Anderson was added to the National Inventors Hall of Fame in the United States, more than a century after she first noticed a struggling driver on a snowy street. Her story is a reminder that great inventions often begin with someone simply paying attention to an everyday problem and refusing to accept that nothing can be done.

=== b2-the-memory-of-water | How the Dead Sea Is Disappearing | Environment | B2
The Dead Sea, located between Jordan, Israel, and the Palestinian West Bank, is one of the most remarkable places on Earth. Its shores are the lowest land on the planet, more than four hundred meters below sea level. Its water is almost ten times saltier than the ocean, so salty that people can float on its surface without any effort, and so salty that almost nothing can live in it, which is how it got its name. For thousands of years, people have visited it for its supposed healing properties, and today it is a popular tourist destination.

However, the Dead Sea is shrinking at an alarming rate. Its surface level has been falling by more than a meter every year, and it has lost a significant portion of its area since the middle of the twentieth century. Some hotels that were built on the shore are now a considerable distance from the water.

The main cause is human activity. The Dead Sea is fed mainly by the Jordan River. Over the past decades, the countries in the region have diverted much of the river's water for drinking and agriculture, in a region where water is extremely scarce. As a result, only a small fraction of the water that once flowed into the Dead Sea now reaches it. In addition, mineral companies evaporate large amounts of Dead Sea water to extract valuable minerals, such as potash, which is used in fertilizers.

The falling water level has created serious problems. As the water retreats, it leaves behind layers of salt underground. When fresh water from rain and underground sources dissolves this salt, large holes suddenly open in the ground. Thousands of these sinkholes have appeared along the shores, swallowing roads, buildings, and farmland, and making some areas too dangerous to visit.

Various solutions have been proposed. One ambitious plan involved building a canal or pipeline to bring water from the Red Sea, but the project faced political, financial, and environmental difficulties. Environmental groups argue that the most effective solution would be to allow more water from the Jordan River to flow into the Dead Sea, which would require cooperation between countries in the region.

The story of the Dead Sea illustrates how the demands of growing populations and economies can transform even the most ancient landscapes, and how protecting shared natural resources often depends on cooperation across borders.

=== b2-the-unfinished-symphony | The Unfinished Song | Short Fiction | B2
For forty years, the old upright piano in the corner of the Martins' living room had remained closed. It had belonged to Robert Martin's wife, Anna, a gifted musician who had taught piano to half the children in the town. After she died, Robert could not bear to hear it, or to sell it. He simply covered it with a cloth and placed a vase of flowers on top.

When his granddaughter, Lucy, came to stay with him for the summer, she was fourteen, quiet, and miserable. Her parents were going through a difficult divorce, and she spent most of her time in her room, wearing headphones. Robert, who was not good at talking about feelings, did not know how to help her.

One afternoon, he came home from the shops and heard music. He stopped in the hallway, his heart pounding. Lucy had removed the cloth from the piano and was playing, hesitantly at first, then with growing confidence. She was playing a piece he had not heard in decades, a gentle, unfamiliar melody that Anna had composed but never finished.

When Lucy noticed him, she stopped, embarrassed. "I'm sorry," she said. "I found this inside the piano stool." She held up a few sheets of handwritten music, yellowed with age. At the top of the first page, in Anna's elegant handwriting, was written: "For Robert. To be continued." The music ended abruptly in the middle of a page.

Robert sat down heavily on the sofa. He told Lucy about Anna, about how she used to play in the evenings, and about the song she had been writing for their fortieth anniversary, the anniversary she had not lived to see. He had never known she had written any of it down. For the first time in years, he cried in front of someone else.

Lucy listened. Then she said, "I could try to finish it." She had been taking piano lessons for six years and had recently started composing. For the rest of the summer, she worked on the song, sometimes asking Robert to describe Anna, what she was like, what made her laugh. On the last evening of Lucy's visit, she played the completed piece for him. It began with Anna's melody and continued into something new, but it still sounded like her.

Robert never covered the piano again. Lucy came back every summer, and every summer, she played the song.

=== b2-the-gene-editing-revolution | Rewriting the Code of Life | Science | B2
In two thousand twelve, two scientists, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier, published a paper describing a new technique for editing genes. The method, known as CRISPR, was based on a natural defense system used by bacteria to fight viruses. It allows scientists to cut DNA at precise locations and to remove, add, or replace sections of genetic code. Compared with earlier methods, CRISPR was much simpler, cheaper, and more accurate. Within a few years, it had transformed biological research, and in two thousand twenty, Doudna and Charpentier were awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

The potential applications of CRISPR are enormous. In medicine, it offers the possibility of treating or even curing genetic diseases caused by errors in a single gene. In two thousand twenty-three, the first CRISPR-based therapy was approved in several countries for treating sickle cell disease, a painful inherited blood disorder. In this treatment, doctors remove stem cells from a patient's bone marrow, edit them in a laboratory, and return them to the patient's body. Many patients who received the treatment no longer experienced the severe pain that had dominated their lives.

Researchers are also exploring the use of CRISPR to treat certain types of cancer, blindness, and other conditions. In agriculture, it could be used to develop crops that are more resistant to disease, drought, or pests, or that are more nutritious. Some scientists have even proposed using gene editing to control populations of mosquitoes that spread diseases such as malaria.

However, the technology also raises profound ethical questions. Most current treatments involve editing cells in a person's body that are not passed on to future generations. Editing the DNA of embryos, eggs, or sperm, by contrast, would create changes that could be inherited by the person's children and grandchildren. In two thousand eighteen, a Chinese scientist shocked the world by announcing that he had edited the genes of twin baby girls before birth. His actions were widely condemned by the scientific community, and he was later sent to prison.

Critics worry that gene editing could one day be used not only to prevent disease but to enhance human abilities, for example to make children taller or more intelligent. This could create new forms of inequality between those who can afford such enhancements and those who cannot. There are also concerns about unintended consequences, since changes to one gene can have unexpected effects. As the technology advances, societies will have to decide how far they are willing to go in rewriting the code of life.

=== b2-the-library-in-the-forest | A Library for the Future | Culture | B2
In a forest just outside Oslo, the capital of Norway, a thousand spruce trees were planted in two thousand fourteen. They are being carefully looked after, and they will not be cut down for a hundred years. When they are finally harvested, in the year twenty one hundred and fourteen, their wood will be turned into paper to print a special collection of books. Nobody alive today, except perhaps some very young children, is likely to read them.

This unusual art project is called the Future Library. It was created by the Scottish artist Katie Paterson. Every year since two thousand fourteen, one author has been invited to write a new book for the project. The authors can write anything they like, of any length, but there is one important condition: the manuscript will not be published or read by anyone until twenty one hundred and fourteen.

Each year, in a special ceremony in the forest, the chosen author hands over their sealed manuscript. The first was the celebrated Canadian novelist Margaret Atwood. Since then, writers from many different countries have contributed, including the British author David Mitchell, the Turkish novelist Elif Shafak, and the South Korean writer Han Kang, who later won the Nobel Prize in Literature. The manuscripts are stored in a special room in Oslo's main public library, where visitors can see the titles of the books and the names of the authors, but not their contents.

For the writers, the project is a unique experience. They know that they will never see their books published or hear what readers think of them. Some have described it as an act of trust and hope, a message to an unknown future. Margaret Atwood said that the project made her think about whether there would still be people, forests, and libraries in a hundred years.

The Future Library is not only about literature. It is also about time, patience, and responsibility. At a time when much of modern life is focused on the immediate, the project asks people to think about the long-term consequences of their actions and the world they will leave to future generations. The forest itself must be protected for a century, which requires the commitment of people who have not yet been born.

A trust has been established to look after the project and ensure that the books are printed in twenty one hundred and fourteen. Until then, the trees continue to grow, and the manuscripts wait silently for their first readers.

=== b2-the-sleep-of-animals | How Animals Sleep | Animals | B2
Almost every animal that scientists have studied appears to sleep in some form. Yet the ways in which different species sleep vary enormously, and some of them are truly extraordinary. Studying animal sleep can help scientists understand why sleep is so important and why it has survived throughout evolution, despite the obvious dangers of being unconscious.

Some animals sleep for a remarkably large part of the day. Brown bats, for example, may sleep for around twenty hours, waking mainly in the evening to hunt insects. Koalas can sleep for up to eighteen or twenty hours a day, partly because their diet of eucalyptus leaves provides very little energy. At the other extreme, adult elephants in the wild may sleep for only about two hours a night, often while standing up.

One of the most fascinating adaptations is unihemispheric sleep, in which one half of the brain sleeps while the other remains awake. Dolphins and some whales sleep in this way, which allows them to continue swimming and to come to the surface to breathe. They can also keep one eye open to watch for predators. Many birds can sleep in the same way, which is particularly useful for birds that sit at the edge of a group, where they are more exposed to danger.

Some birds can even sleep while flying. In a study of frigatebirds, which can spend weeks flying over the ocean without landing, scientists attached small devices to the birds' heads to record their brain activity. They found that the birds slept for very short periods during flight, sometimes with one half of the brain and sometimes with both, but for a total of less than an hour a day, compared with more than twelve hours on land.

Giraffes are also light sleepers. They usually sleep for only a few minutes at a time, often while standing, and their total sleep may be less than five hours a day. Sea otters hold paws with each other while they sleep floating on the water, so that they do not drift apart.

Despite these differences, the widespread presence of sleep across the animal kingdom suggests that it serves essential functions, such as restoring energy, repairing the body, and supporting memory and learning. Some scientists have found that even simple creatures, such as jellyfish and worms, enter sleep-like states. Sleep, it seems, is one of the oldest and most fundamental behaviors of life on Earth.
