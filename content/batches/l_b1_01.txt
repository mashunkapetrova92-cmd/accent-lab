=== b1-the-rise-of-remote-work | Working from Anywhere | Work | B1
Not long ago, working from home was unusual. Most companies expected their employees to come to the office every day, and people who worked from home were often seen as less serious. Then, almost overnight, millions of people around the world had to start working from their kitchens and bedrooms. Many of them have never gone back.

For many workers, remote work has clear advantages. They no longer spend hours every week traveling to and from work, which saves time, money, and stress. They can organize their day more freely, for example by taking a walk at lunchtime or starting early and finishing early. Parents often find it easier to combine work and family life.

Some people have taken this freedom even further. They have become "digital nomads," living and working in different countries while they travel. As long as they have a laptop and a good internet connection, they can work from a beach in Thailand or a mountain village in Spain.

However, remote work is not perfect. Many people feel lonely and miss the social contact of the office. It can be harder to separate work from free time, and some people end up working longer hours than before. Young employees, in particular, may find it difficult to learn from more experienced colleagues when everybody is at home.

That is why many companies now offer a mix of both, known as hybrid work. Employees might spend two or three days in the office and the rest at home. For many people, this seems to be the best of both worlds.

=== b1-the-boy-who-harnessed-the-wind | The Boy Who Caught the Wind | Science | B1
In two thousand one, the small African country of Malawi was hit by a terrible drought. There was no rain, the crops died, and millions of people went hungry. William Kamkwamba was fourteen years old at the time. His family were farmers, and they could no longer pay for him to go to school.

William was disappointed, but he didn't give up on learning. He started going to a small library in his village, where he read every science book he could find. One day, he found a book with a picture of a windmill on the cover. He learned that windmills could produce electricity and pump water. He had an idea: if he could build one, his family could water their fields even when there was no rain.

He had no money and no proper materials, so he collected what he could find in a local scrapyard. He used an old bicycle frame, a tractor fan, plastic pipes, and pieces of wood. The people in his village thought he was crazy. Some even said he was using magic.

But after months of work, his windmill was ready. When the wind blew, the blades turned, and a small light bulb in his house came on. His neighbors were amazed. Soon, people were coming to charge their phones, and William later built a windmill to pump water for the fields.

William's story spread around the world. He was given the chance to return to school and later studied at a university in the United States. He wrote a book about his experience, and it was made into a film. Today, he works on projects that help young people in Africa solve problems in their own communities.

=== b1-the-secret-life-of-octopuses | The Clever Octopus | Animals | B1
The octopus is one of the strangest animals on our planet. It has eight arms, three hearts, and blue blood. It can change the color of its skin in less than a second, and it can squeeze its soft body through a hole as small as a coin. But perhaps the most surprising thing about the octopus is its intelligence.

Scientists have discovered that octopuses can solve complex problems. In experiments, they have learned to open jars to get food inside, and some have even learned to open them from the inside. They can find their way through mazes and remember the solution later. Some octopuses in the wild collect coconut shells and carry them around to use as a portable home.

Octopuses also seem to have individual personalities. Some are shy, while others are curious and bold. In aquariums, they can recognize different people. Some octopuses have been known to squirt water at keepers they don't like. One famous octopus in New Zealand escaped from its tank at night, crossed the floor, and slid down a pipe into the sea.

What makes octopus intelligence so interesting is that it developed in a completely different way from ours. Octopuses are more closely related to snails than to humans. Most of their brain cells are not in their head, but in their arms. This means each arm can taste, touch, and even make simple decisions on its own.

Studying octopuses helps scientists understand that intelligence can take many different forms. It also makes many people think again about how we treat these remarkable animals.

=== b1-the-surprise-visitor | An Unexpected Guest | Short Fiction | B1
It was a stormy night in November, and Martha was alone in her farmhouse. Her husband was away visiting his sister, and the nearest neighbor lived three kilometers down the road. She was sitting by the fire with a book when she heard a knock at the door.

Martha froze. Nobody ever came to the farm at this time of night. The knock came again, louder this time. She picked up the phone, ready to call for help, and walked slowly to the door. "Who's there?" she called.

"I'm very sorry to bother you," said a man's voice. "My car has broken down on the road, and my phone has no signal. I've been walking for an hour. Could I use your phone?" Martha hesitated. Then she looked through the window and saw a young man, soaking wet and shivering, with a little girl in his arms.

She opened the door immediately. She gave them towels and dry clothes, made hot soup, and let them sit by the fire. The man, whose name was David, called a mechanic, but nobody could come until the morning. So Martha made up the spare bed for them.

The next morning, the storm had passed. David's car was repaired, and before he left, he thanked her again and again. Three weeks later, Martha received a parcel. Inside was a beautiful painting of her farmhouse in the storm, with a light shining in the window. On the back, it said: "Thank you for opening your door. From David and Lily."

=== b1-the-benefits-of-boredom | Why Boredom Is Good for You | Psychology | B1
When was the last time you felt really bored? For many of us, it is hard to remember. Today, whenever we have a free moment, while waiting for a bus or standing in a line, we take out our phones and start scrolling. We are never more than a few seconds away from entertainment.

Most people think boredom is something bad that we should avoid. But some psychologists believe that a little boredom can actually be good for us. When our brain has nothing to focus on, it starts to wander. We begin to daydream, to think about our lives, and to make connections between different ideas. This is often when we have our most creative thoughts.

In one famous experiment, researchers asked people to do a very boring task: copying numbers from a phone book. Then they asked them to think of as many uses as possible for a plastic cup. The people who had done the boring task came up with more creative ideas than people who hadn't.

Boredom can also motivate us. When we feel bored, it is often a sign that we need a change. It can push us to try a new hobby, learn a new skill, or look for more meaningful work. Children who have time to be bored often invent their own games and stories.

So the next time you feel bored, try not to reach for your phone immediately. Look out of the window, go for a walk without headphones, or simply sit and let your mind wander. You might be surprised by what you discover.

=== b1-tiny-houses | The Tiny House Movement | Home | B1
Over the last twenty years, houses in many countries have become bigger and bigger. But a growing number of people are choosing to go in the opposite direction. They are moving into tiny houses, homes that are often smaller than twenty square meters.

A tiny house usually has everything a normal house has, just in a much smaller space. There is a kitchen, a bathroom, and a living area. The bed is often in a loft, which you reach by a ladder. Every centimeter is used cleverly. Stairs might have drawers inside them, tables fold away into the wall, and sofas turn into beds.

People choose tiny houses for different reasons. For many, the main reason is money. Houses in many cities have become extremely expensive, and a tiny house costs a fraction of the price. Some owners can buy their home without a loan, which gives them a lot of financial freedom. Others are attracted by the idea of a simpler life, with fewer possessions and less cleaning.

Tiny houses are also better for the environment. They need much less energy to heat and cool, and they are often built with recycled materials. Some have solar panels and collect rainwater. Many tiny houses are built on wheels, so their owners can move them to a new place whenever they like.

But living in a tiny house is not for everyone. There is very little storage space, and it can be difficult to share such a small home with a partner or children. In many places, laws also make it hard to find somewhere to park one legally. Still, for some people, less space really does mean more freedom.

=== b1-the-power-of-music | How Music Affects the Brain | Science | B1
Music is part of every human culture in the world. People sing to their babies, dance at weddings, and listen to music when they are happy, sad, or bored. But why does music have such a strong effect on us? Scientists have been studying this question for many years.

When we listen to music we enjoy, our brain releases dopamine. This is the same chemical that is released when we eat delicious food or fall in love, and it makes us feel good. Interestingly, the brain releases dopamine not only at the best part of a song, but also just before it, while we are waiting for it. This is why the moment before a song's chorus can feel so exciting.

Music also has a strong connection with memory. A song can take us back to a particular summer or a special person in an instant. Doctors have found that people with serious memory problems, such as Alzheimer's disease, can often remember the words of songs from their youth, even when they have forgotten many other things. Some care homes now use music to help patients feel calmer and more connected.

Learning to play an instrument seems to be especially good for the brain. Studies suggest that children who learn music often become better at languages, mathematics, and concentration. For older people, playing music may help keep the brain healthy.

Music can even affect our bodies. Slow, calm music can lower our heart rate and reduce stress, while fast music can give us energy during exercise. It is no surprise that so many people go running with headphones on.

=== b1-a-city-without-cars | A City Center Without Cars | Environment | B1
In nineteen ninety-nine, the city of Pontevedra in northern Spain made a bold decision. It banned private cars from its city center. At the time, many people thought the plan would destroy the local shops and make life difficult for residents. Today, the city is often held up as an example for the rest of the world.

Before the change, the streets were full of traffic, noise, and pollution. Parked cars covered the sidewalks, and children rarely played outside. The mayor believed that streets should be for people, not cars. So the city closed the center to traffic, built large free parking areas on the edge of town, and turned roads into pedestrian areas with trees and benches.

The results were remarkable. Air pollution fell dramatically, and the number of traffic accidents dropped. The center became quiet enough to hear birds singing. Families returned to live in the old town, and children began walking to school on their own again. Instead of closing, many small shops and cafes did better business, because more people were walking past them.

Of course, there are still some cars in the center. Delivery vans, emergency vehicles, and people with disabilities can still drive there, but only slowly. Everybody else walks or rides a bike. Since the city is small, most places can be reached on foot in about fifteen minutes.

Not every city can copy Pontevedra exactly, especially very large ones. But many cities, from Oslo to Paris, are now taking similar steps to reduce cars in their centers and give more space back to people.

=== b1-the-unlikely-friendship | An Unlikely Friendship | Animals | B1
Animals of different species don't usually become friends, especially when one of them normally hunts the other. But every now and then, a story appears that surprises everybody. One of the most famous happened at a wildlife park in Russia.

In two thousand fifteen, the park workers gave a live goat to a Siberian tiger called Amur. The goat was supposed to be his dinner. Tigers in the park were regularly fed live animals so they could practice their hunting skills. But this goat, which was later named Timur, didn't seem to understand that he was in danger. Instead of running away, he walked straight up to the tiger and stood his ground.

Amur seemed confused. He didn't attack. Over the next few days, the two animals began spending time together. They walked around the enclosure side by side, and Timur even started sleeping in Amur's shelter, while the tiger slept outside on the roof. The park workers couldn't believe what they were seeing.

The story quickly spread around the world. People watched videos of the pair online, and thousands of visitors came to the park to see them. Scientists offered different explanations. Some said the goat's lack of fear had confused the tiger. Others pointed out that Amur was well fed and simply wasn't hungry.

The friendship lasted for a few months until Timur became too bold and began to annoy the tiger, who finally lost patience and threw him off a hill. Timur was hurt but survived, and he lived for several more years in a separate enclosure.

=== b1-the-secret-of-long-life | The Secret of a Long Life | Health | B1
In a few places in the world, people live much longer than average. Many of them reach the age of one hundred, and they often stay healthy and active until the end of their lives. Researchers call these places "Blue Zones," and they have been studying them to find out what their residents have in common.

The Blue Zones include the island of Okinawa in Japan, a mountain region on the Italian island of Sardinia, and a small town in California. The people who live there have very different cultures, but their lifestyles share some important features.

First, they don't do intense exercise, but they move naturally all day. They walk, work in their gardens, and do physical jobs at home. Second, they eat mostly plants, such as beans, vegetables, and whole grains, and only small amounts of meat. Many of them also stop eating before they feel completely full. In Okinawa, people have a saying that reminds them to stop eating when they are about eighty percent full.

But the secrets are not only about diet and exercise. People in Blue Zones usually have strong connections with their family and community. They spend time with friends and often belong to a religious or social group. They also have a clear sense of purpose. In Okinawa, there is a word for this, "ikigai," which means a reason to get up in the morning.

Of course, genes play a role in how long we live. But researchers believe that lifestyle is even more important. The good news is that many of these habits are simple and free.

=== b1-the-forgotten-language | Saving a Language | Culture | B1
There are around seven thousand languages spoken in the world today. But experts believe that about half of them could disappear by the end of this century. Every two weeks or so, the last speaker of a language dies, and that language is lost forever.

Languages usually disappear when a community starts using a more powerful language, such as English, Spanish, or Chinese. Parents decide to speak the dominant language with their children, because they think it will give them better opportunities. Within a few generations, only old people speak the traditional language, and then nobody does.

When a language dies, much more than words is lost. Every language contains a unique way of looking at the world. It holds stories, songs, jokes, and knowledge about plants, animals, and medicine that may not exist anywhere else. Some languages have words for ideas that other languages cannot easily express.

Fortunately, some languages have been brought back from the edge. One famous example is the Welsh language in Britain. In the middle of the twentieth century, the number of speakers was falling fast. Then Welsh began to be taught in schools, and there are now Welsh television channels and radio stations. Today, hundreds of thousands of people speak it. In New Zealand, the Maori people created "language nests," where young children spend their days with older people who speak only Maori.

Technology can help too. Linguists are recording the last speakers of endangered languages, and some communities are creating apps and online courses to teach their language to young people.

=== b1-the-detective-dog | The Dog with an Amazing Nose | Animals | B1
Dogs have been helping humans for thousands of years. They have guarded our homes, herded our sheep, and kept us company. Today, they are also doing some surprising jobs, thanks to one special ability: their amazing sense of smell.

A dog's nose is incredibly powerful. While humans have about six million smell receptors in their noses, some dogs have around three hundred million. The part of a dog's brain that analyzes smells is also much larger than ours. Scientists believe that dogs can smell some things at concentrations a hundred thousand times lower than we can.

Because of this, dogs are trained to do many important jobs. At airports, they sniff bags to find drugs, explosives, or food that people are not allowed to bring into the country. After earthquakes, rescue dogs search through buildings to find people trapped under the ruins. Other dogs help to find people who are lost in forests or mountains.

Even more surprisingly, some dogs can detect illnesses. Researchers have trained dogs to recognize the smell of certain types of cancer, sometimes before doctors can find it with tests. Other dogs live with people who have diabetes. They can smell when their owner's blood sugar is dangerously low and warn them before they feel ill.

Training a dog for this kind of work takes a long time, often more than a year. Not every dog is suitable. The best ones are curious, energetic, and love to play. For them, finding a smell isn't work at all. It is a game, and the reward is usually a favorite toy.

=== b1-the-power-of-kindness | Small Acts of Kindness | Society | B1
Most of us like to think of ourselves as kind people. But in our busy lives, it is easy to forget about the small things we can do for others. Research suggests that these small acts of kindness are good not only for the people who receive them, but also for the people who do them.

In one study, psychologists gave people some money and asked them to spend it. Half of the group had to spend it on themselves, and the other half had to spend it on somebody else. At the end of the day, the people who had spent the money on others felt happier, even though the amount was small.

Doing something kind for others can also reduce stress and improve our health. When we help somebody, our brain releases chemicals that make us feel good and calm. Some scientists call this the "helper's high." People who volunteer regularly often report feeling more satisfied with their lives.

Kindness can also spread. When someone is kind to us, we are more likely to be kind to the next person we meet. A simple smile, a thank you note, or holding a door open can start a chain of positive actions. In one coffee shop in the United States, a customer paid for the drink of the person behind them in line. That person did the same, and the chain continued for more than a hundred people.

You don't need to do something big. Calling a lonely relative, helping a neighbor with their shopping, or listening to a friend who is having a bad day can make a real difference.

=== b1-the-first-day-at-a-new-job | First Day Nerves | Work | B1
Daniel had been looking for a job for eight months when he finally got an offer from a small design company. He was thrilled, but also extremely nervous. The night before his first day, he hardly slept. He kept imagining all the things that could go wrong.

He arrived at the office thirty minutes early, wearing his best shirt. The receptionist smiled and told him to wait. While he was sitting there, he spilled coffee all over his pants. He rushed to the bathroom and tried to clean them, but it only made things worse. When he came out, there was a large wet stain on his leg.

His new manager, a woman called Grace, came to meet him. Daniel was so embarrassed that he started explaining immediately. Grace laughed. "On my first day here, I locked myself out of the building and had to climb through a window," she said. "Don't worry. Coffee is the least of your problems."

When he got home that evening, his girlfriend asked how it had gone. Daniel thought for a moment. "Terrible start," he said with a smile. "But I think I'm going to like it there." Two years later, he still works at the same company, and every new employee hears the story of the coffee.

=== b1-the-great-wall | The Great Wall of China | History | B1
The Great Wall of China is one of the most famous structures in the world. Every year, millions of tourists visit it, walking along its ancient stones and admiring the views of the mountains around it. But there are many myths about the wall, and its true history is even more interesting.

First of all, the Great Wall is not a single wall. It is a series of walls built over a period of more than two thousand years by different Chinese dynasties. The earliest parts were built around the seventh century before Christ. Most of the wall that tourists see today was built during the Ming Dynasty, between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries.

The wall was built mainly to protect China from attacks by groups from the north. It had watchtowers where soldiers could look out for enemies. When they saw danger, they lit fires or sent smoke signals to warn the next tower. In this way, a message could travel hundreds of kilometers in just a few hours.

Building the wall was extremely hard work. Millions of soldiers, farmers, and prisoners worked on it, often in terrible conditions. Many of them died during the construction. In the mountains, workers had to carry heavy stones up steep slopes by hand.

One popular myth says that the Great Wall can be seen from the Moon. This is not true. Astronauts have said that it is very difficult to see even from a low orbit around the Earth, because it is narrow and has a similar color to the land around it.

=== b1-coffee-around-the-world | Coffee Around the World | Culture | B1
Coffee is one of the most popular drinks on the planet. People drink more than two billion cups of it every day. But the way people drink coffee is very different from one country to another.

The coffee plant probably came from Ethiopia, in East Africa. According to a popular legend, a goat herder noticed that his goats became very energetic after eating red berries from a certain bush. In Ethiopia today, coffee is still part of an important ceremony. The beans are roasted, ground, and boiled in a special pot in front of the guests. The ceremony can take more than an hour, and it is a time for talking and friendship.

In Italy, most people drink espresso, a small, strong coffee. Italians usually drink it standing at the bar, and it is finished in a minute or two. Many Italians believe that you should never drink a cappuccino, which contains a lot of milk, after about eleven o'clock in the morning.

In Turkey, coffee is boiled with sugar in a small pot and served without being filtered, so the coffee grounds stay at the bottom of the cup. In Vietnam, a popular drink is iced coffee with sweet condensed milk. Some cafes there also serve egg coffee, which is topped with a sweet, creamy egg foam.

In the United States, people often drink large cups of filter coffee, and many take their coffee with them to drink on the way to work. Whatever the style, coffee brings people together in almost every culture.

=== b1-the-message-in-a-bottle | The Message in a Bottle | Short Fiction | B1
Ten-year-old Ethan was walking along the beach with his grandfather when he saw something shining in the sand. It was an old green bottle with a cork in the top. Inside, there was a rolled-up piece of paper.

Ethan's grandfather helped him pull out the cork. The paper was yellow and fragile, and the handwriting was faded, but they could still read it. "My name is Anna," it said. "I am nine years old, and I live in a small town in Norway. If you find this bottle, please write to me." There was an address at the bottom. The letter was dated more than fifty years ago.

Ethan was fascinated. "Do you think she is still alive?" he asked. "She would be an old woman now," his grandfather said. "But let's try." That evening, they wrote a letter and sent it to the address, not really expecting an answer.

Six weeks later, a letter arrived from Norway. It was from Anna's granddaughter. She explained that her grandmother still lived in the same town and was now sixty-three. She remembered throwing the bottle into the sea from her father's fishing boat, and she was amazed that it had traveled so far.

Ethan and Anna started writing to each other regularly. They sent photos and told each other about their lives. Two summers later, Ethan's family traveled to Norway, and Ethan met Anna in person. She took him out on a boat, and together they threw a new bottle into the sea.

=== b1-the-science-of-happiness | What Makes Us Happy? | Psychology | B1
Everybody wants to be happy, but what actually makes us happy? Many people believe that money, success, or a beautiful house will make them happier. But according to researchers who study happiness, the truth is a little more complicated.

Money does matter, up to a point. People who don't have enough money for food, housing, and healthcare are usually less happy, because they live with constant stress. But once people have enough to live comfortably, extra money doesn't increase their happiness very much. A person who earns twice as much as their neighbor is not usually twice as happy.

One of the longest studies of happiness began at an American university in the nineteen thirties. Researchers followed hundreds of men for more than eighty years, and later their children too. The most important finding was clear: the people who were happiest and healthiest in old age were the ones with good relationships. Close friendships and a strong family were more important than money, fame, or career success.

Other research has shown that we often get used to good things quickly. When people buy a new car or move to a bigger house, they feel happy for a while, but soon it becomes normal. Experiences, such as traveling or learning something new, tend to give us more lasting happiness than possessions.

Experts also say that small daily habits can make a difference. Spending time outside, exercising regularly, getting enough sleep, and helping others can all improve our mood. Some people also find it helpful to write down three things they are grateful for every evening.

=== b1-the-volcano-island | The Island That Appeared from the Sea | Science | B1
In November nineteen sixty-three, fishermen near the south coast of Iceland noticed something strange. Black smoke was rising out of the sea. At first, they thought a ship was on fire. But when they got closer, they saw that it was something much more unusual. A volcano was erupting under the water.

Over the next few days, the eruption grew stronger. Huge clouds of ash and steam rose kilometers into the sky. Then, a few days later, a small black island appeared above the waves. The volcano continued to erupt for more than three years, and by the end, the new island was more than two square kilometers in size. It was named Surtsey, after a giant from old Icelandic stories.

Scientists immediately realized that Surtsey was a unique opportunity. Here was a completely new piece of land, with no plants or animals at all. They could watch how life arrived and developed on it, from the very beginning. To protect the island, the government made it a nature reserve. Only a small number of scientists are allowed to visit it.

Life arrived surprisingly quickly. Within two years, the first plant was growing on the beach. Its seeds had probably been carried there by the sea. Birds started to visit, bringing more seeds on their feet and in their droppings. Today, dozens of plant species grow on Surtsey, and many birds nest there.

However, the sea is slowly eating away at the island. Scientists think that in a hundred years, much of Surtsey may disappear back under the waves.

=== b1-online-friendships | Can You Make Real Friends Online? | Technology | B1
Twenty years ago, if you told people that your best friend was somebody you had met on the internet, they might have thought it was strange. Today, it is very common. Millions of people have friends they met through online games, social media, forums, or language exchange apps.

Online friendships have some clear advantages. The internet allows us to connect with people who share our interests, even if those interests are unusual. If you love old science fiction films or a rare type of music, you might not find anybody in your town who shares your passion. Online, you can find thousands. For people who are shy, live in isolated places, or have health problems that make it hard to go out, online friends can be especially important.

Some people also find it easier to be honest online. Without seeing the other person's face, they feel less judged, so they share their thoughts and feelings more openly. Many online friendships become very deep.

However, there are also risks. It is easy for people to pretend to be someone they are not. Some people spend so much time online that they neglect their friends and family in real life. And some research suggests that online communication, while valuable, cannot completely replace the experience of being with people face to face.

Many friendships that start online eventually move offline. Friends meet at concerts or events, or travel to visit each other. Perhaps the most important thing is not where a friendship begins, but how much care and honesty both people put into it.
