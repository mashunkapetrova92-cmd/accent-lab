=== b2-the-loneliness-epidemic | The Hidden Epidemic of Loneliness | Society | B2
We live in the most connected age in human history. With a smartphone, we can send messages to friends on the other side of the world in seconds, join online communities of people who share our interests, and see what hundreds of acquaintances are doing at any moment. And yet, according to many researchers, loneliness is becoming one of the most serious public health problems of our time.

Surveys in several countries suggest that a significant proportion of adults feel lonely often or always. While loneliness is commonly associated with older people, who may have lost partners and friends, studies have found that young adults are now among the loneliest groups of all.

It is important to distinguish between being alone and feeling lonely. Many people enjoy spending time on their own and feel perfectly content. Loneliness is the painful feeling that our relationships do not meet our needs, either because we have too few of them or because they lack depth. It is possible to feel lonely in a crowded room, or even within a marriage.

The effects of chronic loneliness on health can be severe. Research has linked it to higher risks of heart disease, stroke, depression, and dementia. Some studies have even suggested that long-term loneliness can be as harmful to health as smoking fifteen cigarettes a day. Lonely people also tend to sleep less well and have weaker immune systems.

The causes are complex. Changes in society play a role: more people live alone than ever before, families are often spread across different cities, and many people move frequently for work. Some experts believe that social media, while helping us stay in touch, may also replace deeper, face-to-face contact.

In response, some governments have begun to treat loneliness as a serious political issue. The United Kingdom appointed a minister for loneliness in two thousand eighteen, and Japan followed a few years later. Doctors in some countries can now refer patients to community activities, such as walking groups, art classes, or volunteering, a practice known as social prescribing.

On an individual level, small actions can help. Reaching out to an old friend, talking to a neighbor, or joining a club can be the first step toward building connections. And noticing when others might be lonely, and taking the time to listen, can make a bigger difference than we realize.

=== b2-the-forgotten-genius | The Woman Behind the Double Helix | Science | B2
In nineteen fifty-three, two scientists at the University of Cambridge, James Watson and Francis Crick, announced that they had discovered the structure of DNA, the molecule that carries the genetic instructions for all living things. Their model of the double helix, a twisted ladder, became one of the most famous images in science. In nineteen sixty-two, Watson and Crick received the Nobel Prize, together with a colleague named Maurice Wilkins. But one name was missing: Rosalind Franklin.

Franklin was a brilliant chemist who worked at King's College in London. She was an expert in a technique called X-ray crystallography, which uses X-rays to reveal the structure of molecules. Working with a student, she produced extremely clear images of DNA. One of them, known as Photo Fifty-one, provided crucial evidence that DNA had a helical shape.

What happened next has been the subject of controversy for decades. Without Franklin's knowledge, Wilkins showed Photo Fifty-one to Watson. Watson later wrote that when he saw it, his mouth fell open and his heart began to race. Watson and Crick also saw a report containing Franklin's unpublished data. This information helped them to build their model.

Franklin's working life at King's College was not easy. She faced a male-dominated environment in which women were often excluded. She had a difficult relationship with Wilkins, who sometimes treated her as an assistant rather than an equal colleague.

Tragically, Franklin died of cancer in nineteen fifty-eight, at the age of only thirty-seven. Because the Nobel Prize is not awarded after death, she could not have shared the prize in nineteen sixty-two. Nevertheless, for many years, her contribution was largely ignored. In his famous book about the discovery, Watson described her in an unflattering and sexist way.

In recent decades, Franklin's reputation has been restored. Historians have recognized the importance of her work, and she has become a symbol of the many women scientists whose achievements were overlooked. Schools, research institutes, and even a Mars rover have been named after her. Her story is a reminder that scientific discoveries are rarely the work of just one or two people.

=== b2-the-sound-of-a-city | What Does Your City Sound Like? | Places | B2
Close your eyes and listen. If you are in a city, you might hear traffic, sirens, construction work, and the hum of air conditioning units. You might also hear birdsong, children playing, or the bells of a distant church. Every city has its own soundscape, and some researchers believe that paying more attention to it could make our cities healthier and more pleasant places to live.

For a long time, city planners treated sound mainly as a problem to be reduced. Noise from traffic, airports, and industry has been linked to stress, sleep disturbance, and heart disease. The World Health Organization considers environmental noise one of the most significant environmental threats to health in Europe. Many cities have introduced noise maps, quieter road surfaces, and barriers alongside highways.

However, a new approach, sometimes called soundscape design, argues that simply reducing noise is not enough. Instead, it asks what sounds people actually want to hear. Studies have found that people generally find natural sounds, such as water and birdsong, relaxing and pleasant. The sound of a fountain in a busy square, for example, can partially mask traffic noise and make the space feel calmer, even if the overall noise level is similar.

Some cities have experimented with creative sound installations. Musical benches, singing bridges, and speakers that play recordings of nature in underground stations are all attempts to improve urban soundscapes. In one city, a pedestrian tunnel that people avoided because it felt threatening was transformed by adding gentle music, which made people feel safer.

Interestingly, a completely silent city would probably not feel comfortable either. Some background sound helps us feel connected to the life around us. Many people who moved to the countryside during the pandemic reported finding the silence unsettling at first. And the sounds of a particular place, such as the street calls of a market or the noise of a ferry, can become an important part of its identity and of people's memories.

As cities continue to grow, the way they sound may become as important as the way they look. Designing cities with our ears, as well as our eyes, could help to create places where people genuinely want to live.

=== b2-the-unread-letter | The Letter in the Book | Short Fiction | B2
Maya worked in a secondhand bookshop that smelled of dust and coffee, and she had long ago stopped being surprised by the things people left in books. Train tickets, pressed flowers, shopping lists, and once, memorably, a fifty-dollar bill. But the envelope she found one rainy Tuesday in a copy of an old poetry collection was different. It was sealed, stamped, and addressed in elegant handwriting to a woman named Ruth Hale. It had never been sent.

The date on the stamp was from forty years earlier. Maya held it up to the light, then put it down, embarrassed by her curiosity. She knew she shouldn't open it. But she also knew that if she simply put it back on the shelf, it would never reach the person it had been written for.

She spent her lunch breaks that week searching online. The address on the envelope belonged to a house that had been demolished long ago. There were dozens of women called Ruth Hale. Eventually, through an old newspaper article, she found a Ruth Hale who had been a music teacher in the same town and who, at eighty-one, now lived in a care home on the other side of the city.

Maya visited on a Saturday afternoon. Ruth was small and sharp-eyed, and she looked at the envelope for a long time without touching it. "I know this handwriting," she said finally. "His name was Daniel. We were engaged, and then one day he just stopped calling. I always thought he'd changed his mind."

With trembling fingers, she opened the envelope and read the letter. Maya watched her face change. When Ruth looked up, her eyes were wet. "He was offered a job abroad," she said. "He wanted me to come with him. He asked me to meet him at the station if the answer was yes." She laughed softly. "I never knew. I thought he'd left without saying goodbye."

They sat together in silence while the rain ran down the window. Before Maya left, Ruth pressed her hand. "Thank you," she said. "You've given me back forty years. I used to think he didn't love me. Now I know that he did." Maya walked back to the bookshop slowly, and for the rest of the day, she opened every book with a little more care.

=== b2-the-future-of-food | What Will We Eat in Twenty Years? | Food | B2
By the middle of this century, the world's population is expected to reach nearly ten billion people. Feeding all of them, while protecting the environment, is one of the greatest challenges facing humanity. Scientists, farmers, and entrepreneurs are exploring a range of solutions, some of which may dramatically change what ends up on our plates.

One of the main concerns is meat. Producing meat, particularly beef, requires huge amounts of land and water and generates a significant share of global greenhouse gas emissions. As incomes rise around the world, the demand for meat is increasing. For this reason, many researchers are looking for alternatives.

Plant-based meat substitutes have become increasingly sophisticated. Some burgers made from peas, soy, or other plants are now so similar to beef in taste and texture that many people find it difficult to tell the difference. Another, more radical idea is cultivated meat, which is grown from animal cells in a laboratory without the need to raise and slaughter animals. The first cultivated chicken has already been approved for sale in a few countries, although it remains expensive and is produced in small quantities.

Other possible foods of the future include insects, which are rich in protein and can be farmed with very little land, and algae, which grow quickly and can be turned into a variety of products. Some companies are even developing protein made by microorganisms using little more than air, water, and electricity.

Technology is also changing the way we grow plants. Vertical farms grow crops indoors under artificial light, while scientists are developing new varieties of crops that can survive droughts and floods, which are likely to become more common as the climate changes.

However, many experts emphasize that high-tech solutions alone will not be enough. Roughly a third of all food produced in the world is currently lost or wasted. Reducing this waste, improving the efficiency of existing farms, and helping small farmers in developing countries could make an enormous difference. The future of food may depend as much on changing our habits as on inventing new technologies.

=== b2-the-language-of-whales | Learning to Speak Whale | Animals | B2
Sperm whales are among the most mysterious animals in the ocean. They dive to depths of more than a thousand meters in search of squid, spending up to an hour in complete darkness. They live in close family groups, mostly made up of females and their young, and they communicate using rapid sequences of clicks, known as codas. For decades, scientists have wondered whether these clicks might be something like a language.

Now, an ambitious international project is attempting to answer that question. Called Project CETI, it brings together marine biologists, linguists, roboticists, and experts in artificial intelligence. Their goal is to record vast amounts of sperm whale communication and use machine learning to analyze it, in the hope of understanding what the whales are saying.

The project is based in the Caribbean, near the island of Dominica, where a population of sperm whales has been studied for many years. Researchers have discovered that different groups of whales use different sets of codas, a little like human dialects. Young whales appear to learn these codas from their families, rather than being born knowing them.

Recent research has revealed that sperm whale communication may be more complex than previously thought. Scientists have identified subtle variations in the timing and rhythm of the clicks, which could allow the whales to combine a relatively small number of basic sounds into a much larger range of messages. Some researchers have compared this to the way humans combine a small number of sounds to create thousands of words.

Of course, enormous challenges remain. Even if scientists can identify patterns in whale communication, interpreting their meaning is a different matter. Whales live in a world very different from ours, and their experiences and concerns may be difficult for humans to imagine.

Nevertheless, the project raises fascinating possibilities. If we could understand even a little of what whales are communicating, it might change how we think about animal intelligence and our relationship with the natural world. It could also strengthen arguments for protecting whales and their ocean habitats. As one researcher put it, the aim is not only to listen to whales, but to learn to take them seriously.

=== b2-the-burnout-generation | Understanding Burnout | Work | B2
Most people feel tired and stressed at work from time to time. But for some, these feelings become so intense and persistent that they can no longer function properly. They feel completely exhausted, cynical about their job, and convinced that nothing they do makes a difference. This condition is known as burnout, and in recent years it has become a major topic of discussion.

In two thousand nineteen, the World Health Organization officially recognized burnout in its international classification of diseases, although it describes it as an occupational phenomenon rather than a medical condition. According to the definition, burnout results from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It has three main features: feelings of exhaustion, increasing mental distance from one's job, and reduced effectiveness at work.

Burnout differs from ordinary stress. When we are stressed, we often feel that if we could just get everything under control, we would feel better. People suffering from burnout, by contrast, often feel empty and unable to care. A long vacation may help temporarily, but the symptoms frequently return as soon as they go back to work.

Researchers have identified several factors that increase the risk of burnout. These include an excessive workload, a lack of control over one's work, insufficient recognition or reward, a lack of support from colleagues, a sense of unfairness, and a mismatch between personal values and the values of the organization. In other words, burnout is not simply a sign of personal weakness. It is often a response to problems in the workplace itself.

Certain professions, such as healthcare, teaching, and social work, have particularly high rates of burnout, because they combine heavy workloads with emotionally demanding work. The spread of smartphones and remote working has also made it harder for many people to switch off, as work emails and messages follow them home.

Preventing burnout requires action from both individuals and employers. Individuals can benefit from setting clear boundaries, taking regular breaks, getting enough sleep, and seeking support early. But experts emphasize that organizations must also take responsibility, by ensuring reasonable workloads, giving employees more control, and creating a culture where people feel valued and able to speak up.

=== b2-the-great-fire | The Great Fire of London | History | B2
In the early hours of Sunday, the second of September, sixteen sixty-six, a fire broke out in a bakery on Pudding Lane, a narrow street near London Bridge. The baker, Thomas Farriner, and his family escaped through an upstairs window, but their maid was unable to get out and became the first victim. Nobody could have imagined that over the next four days, the fire would destroy most of the medieval city.

Conditions were perfect for a disaster. The summer had been long and dry, and London's crowded streets were lined with timber houses, many of them built so close together that their upper floors almost touched. Warehouses near the river were full of flammable goods, including oil, tar, and coal. A strong wind from the east carried sparks from building to building.

At first, the authorities did not take the fire seriously. The Lord Mayor, who was woken and taken to see it, reportedly dismissed it as minor. By the time people realized the danger, it was too late. The usual method of stopping fires was to pull down houses to create gaps, but this was not done quickly enough, partly because property owners objected.

The fire spread rapidly. Thousands of people fled with whatever possessions they could carry, crowding onto boats on the river or escaping to the fields outside the city. Much of what we know about these events comes from the diary of Samuel Pepys, a government official, who described the terrifying scenes and famously buried his wine and cheese in his garden to protect them.

The fire was finally brought under control on the fifth of September, helped by a change in the wind and the use of gunpowder to blow up houses. By then, it had destroyed more than thirteen thousand houses, nearly ninety churches, and the great Saint Paul's Cathedral. Around seventy thousand people were left homeless. Officially, only a handful of deaths were recorded, although historians believe the true number was probably much higher.

The disaster led to significant changes. London was rebuilt with brick and stone instead of wood, and streets were made wider. The architect Christopher Wren designed a new Saint Paul's Cathedral and dozens of new churches, many of which still stand today.

=== b2-why-we-believe-conspiracy-theories | Why Do People Believe Conspiracy Theories? | Psychology | B2
The moon landings were faked. A secret group of powerful people controls world events. Governments are hiding evidence of alien visitors. Conspiracy theories like these have existed for centuries, but the internet has allowed them to spread faster and further than ever before. Surveys suggest that a large proportion of people believe in at least one conspiracy theory. So why do so many people find them convincing?

Psychologists have identified several needs that conspiracy theories seem to satisfy. The first is the need for understanding. When something frightening or confusing happens, such as a pandemic or the sudden death of a famous person, people want an explanation. Official explanations can seem complicated or unsatisfying, especially when events are partly due to chance. A conspiracy theory offers a simple story in which everything happens for a reason.

The second is the need for control. When people feel powerless or anxious, believing that they have discovered a hidden truth can make them feel more in control of their situation. Research has shown that people are more likely to believe conspiracy theories during periods of uncertainty and crisis.

The third is the need to feel good about ourselves and the groups we belong to. Believing that we know something that others don't can make us feel special and intelligent. Conspiracy theories can also provide a sense of community, connecting people who share the same beliefs.

Our brains also play a role. Humans are naturally inclined to see patterns, even where none exist. We also tend to assume that big events must have big causes. And once we believe something, we tend to notice evidence that supports our view and ignore evidence against it, a tendency known as confirmation bias.

It is important to note that some conspiracies have turned out to be real, which makes the topic more complicated. However, most popular conspiracy theories are not supported by evidence and can have harmful consequences, for example when they lead people to reject vaccines or distrust democratic institutions.

Researchers suggest that simply mocking believers or bombarding them with facts rarely works. A more effective approach may be to listen respectfully, ask questions that encourage critical thinking, and teach people, especially young people, how to evaluate sources of information.

=== b2-the-hidden-life-of-trees | Do Trees Talk to Each Other? | Nature | B2
When we walk through a forest, we tend to see the trees as individuals, each one competing with its neighbors for sunlight, water, and nutrients. But research over the past few decades has suggested that forests may be far more cooperative and connected than we once believed. Beneath our feet, it seems, trees may be communicating and even sharing resources.

The key to this hidden network lies in fungi. Many trees form partnerships with fungi that live in the soil and wrap around their roots. The fungi produce extremely fine threads that spread through the soil, connecting the roots of different trees. Through this network, the fungi supply trees with water and nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus. In return, the trees provide the fungi with sugars that they produce through photosynthesis. Some scientists have nicknamed this network the "wood wide web."

Experiments by the Canadian ecologist Suzanne Simard and others have suggested that carbon, in the form of sugars, can travel between trees through these fungal connections. Some studies found that larger, older trees, sometimes called "mother trees," appear to share resources with young seedlings growing in their shade. There is also evidence that when a tree is attacked by insects, it may release chemical signals, some of which travel through the air and possibly through the fungal network, prompting neighboring trees to strengthen their defenses.

These ideas have captured the public imagination, and they have been popularized in bestselling books and documentaries. However, some scientists have recently urged caution. They argue that while fungal networks certainly exist, the evidence that trees deliberately share resources or help their offspring is weaker than often claimed. Some of the most famous experiments, they say, have been misinterpreted or exaggerated.

Whatever the final conclusions, the research has changed the way many people think about forests. Rather than collections of isolated individuals, forests can be seen as complex communities, with relationships we are only beginning to understand. This has practical implications. Some foresters now argue that leaving older trees standing when cutting forests, and protecting soil fungi, could help forests recover more quickly and resist disease and drought.

=== b2-the-minimalist-life | Less Is More | Modern Life | B2
Many of us live surrounded by things. Our closets are full of clothes we rarely wear, our kitchens contain gadgets we used once, and our garages are packed with boxes we have not opened in years. For a growing number of people, this excess has become a burden rather than a pleasure. In response, they are embracing minimalism, a lifestyle based on owning fewer possessions and focusing on what truly matters.

Minimalism is not a new idea. Philosophers and religious traditions have praised simple living for thousands of years. But in recent years, it has become a popular movement, spread by books, documentaries, and social media. One of the most famous advocates is the Japanese organizing consultant Marie Kondo, who encourages people to keep only the items that "spark joy."

Supporters of minimalism describe a range of benefits. With fewer possessions, homes are easier to clean and organize, and people spend less time searching for things. Many minimalists say they feel less stressed and more in control of their lives. Buying less can also save a considerable amount of money, which some people use to pay off debts, travel, or work fewer hours.

There is an environmental argument too. Every product we buy requires raw materials and energy to produce, transport, and eventually dispose of. Consuming less reduces this impact. Some minimalists also say that their lifestyle has made them more aware of where their possessions come from and how they are made.

However, minimalism has also attracted criticism. Some critics argue that it has become just another trend, with expensive designer furniture and carefully staged photographs of empty white rooms. They point out that choosing to own less is a luxury available mainly to people who could easily buy more if they needed it. For people living in poverty, having few possessions is not a lifestyle choice.

Perhaps the most useful lesson of minimalism is not about counting possessions, but about being intentional. Asking ourselves whether something really adds value to our lives before we buy it, and letting go of things that no longer serve us, can help anyone, whether they live in a tiny apartment or a large house.

=== b2-the-champion-who-refused | The Runner Who Stopped to Help | Sport | B2
In December two thousand twelve, a cross-country race was taking place in the Basque region of Spain. Near the end of the course, the Kenyan runner Abel Mutai was in the lead, well ahead of his nearest rival, a young Spanish athlete called Ivan Fernandez Anaya. Mutai was an experienced athlete who had won a bronze medal at the Olympic Games in London only a few months earlier. Victory seemed certain.

But then something unexpected happened. Mutai, who did not speak Spanish, misunderstood the signs and the shouts of the crowd. Believing he had already crossed the finish line, he slowed down and stopped about ten meters before the end of the race.

Fernandez Anaya quickly realized what had happened. He could easily have run past Mutai and taken the victory for himself. Instead, he ran up behind the Kenyan, and using gestures, guided him toward the finish line, allowing him to win.

After the race, journalists asked Fernandez Anaya why he had not taken advantage of his rival's mistake. His answer was simple. "He was the rightful winner," he said. "He created a gap that I couldn't have closed if he hadn't made a mistake. As soon as I saw he was stopping, I knew I wasn't going to pass him." He added that winning in that way would have brought him no honor.

His coach was reportedly disappointed, arguing that a victory would have helped the young athlete's career. Some people agreed that in competitive sport, taking advantage of an opponent's error is part of the game. But many others around the world were moved by his decision. The story spread rapidly online, and Fernandez Anaya received thousands of messages praising his sportsmanship.

The incident raises interesting questions about the meaning of success. In a world where winning is often seen as everything, Fernandez Anaya's choice reminded many people that how we win can matter as much as whether we win. As he later said in an interview, "I think I have earned more of a name having done what I did than if I had won."

=== b2-the-space-junk-problem | The Problem of Space Junk | Science | B2
When we look up at the night sky, it appears calm and empty. But the space around our planet is becoming increasingly crowded. Thousands of active satellites now orbit the Earth, providing services that we depend on every day, from weather forecasts and navigation systems to television broadcasts and internet connections. Alongside them, however, is a growing cloud of debris, commonly known as space junk.

Space junk includes old satellites that no longer work, used rocket stages, and countless fragments created by collisions and explosions. According to space agencies, there are tens of thousands of pieces of debris larger than ten centimeters, and millions of smaller fragments. These objects travel at extremely high speeds, often around twenty-eight thousand kilometers an hour. At such speeds, even a tiny piece of metal can cause serious damage.

The danger became clear in two thousand nine, when an inactive Russian satellite crashed into a working American communications satellite, destroying both and creating thousands of new fragments. Some tests of anti-satellite weapons, in which countries have deliberately destroyed their own satellites with missiles, have added thousands more pieces.

The International Space Station regularly has to change its orbit to avoid debris, and astronauts have occasionally had to take shelter in their spacecraft when a collision seemed possible. Some scientists fear a scenario known as the Kessler syndrome, in which collisions create so much debris that they trigger a chain reaction of further collisions. In the worst case, certain orbits could become unusable for generations.

The problem is likely to grow, as companies are launching thousands of new satellites to provide global internet coverage. Several solutions are being explored. New rules require satellites to be removed from orbit within a certain period after the end of their missions, either by moving them into a higher "graveyard" orbit or by bringing them down to burn up in the atmosphere. Engineers are also developing technologies to capture debris, using nets, harpoons, magnets, and even robotic arms.

None of these solutions is simple or cheap, and international cooperation is essential, because space belongs to no single country. But as our dependence on satellites increases, keeping space clean is becoming a necessity rather than a choice.

=== b2-a-room-with-no-view | The Night Shift | Short Fiction | B2

He liked the silence of the long corridors, the soft hum of the air conditioning, and the way the skeletons of the dinosaurs seemed almost alive in the dim emergency lighting. === b2-the-economics-of-happiness | Can Money Buy Happiness? | Business | B2
The question of whether money can buy happiness has been debated for centuries. Philosophers have warned against the love of money, while advertising constantly suggests that the right purchase will make us happier. In recent decades, economists and psychologists have tried to answer the question scientifically, and their findings are both surprising and nuanced.

One of the most influential studies was carried out in the nineteen seventies by the economist Richard Easterlin. He found that within a country, richer people tended to be happier than poorer people. However, as countries became richer over time, average happiness did not seem to increase. This apparent contradiction became known as the Easterlin paradox.

One explanation is that happiness depends partly on how we compare ourselves to others. If everybody's income rises, our relative position stays the same, so we may not feel any better off. Another explanation is that people quickly adapt to improvements in their circumstances. A pay raise or a new car may bring a burst of pleasure, but soon it becomes normal, and our expectations rise. Psychologists call this the hedonic treadmill.

For many years, a popular study suggested that happiness stopped increasing once people earned around seventy-five thousand dollars a year. More recent research, however, has challenged this. It suggests that for most people, life satisfaction continues to rise with income, although the effect becomes smaller as people get richer. Having more money seems to reduce stress and give people more control over their lives.

Perhaps more important than how much money we have is how we spend it. Research suggests that spending money on experiences, such as trips, concerts, or meals with friends, tends to bring more lasting happiness than spending it on material possessions. Spending money on other people, whether by buying gifts or giving to charity, also appears to make people happier than spending it on themselves. Buying time, for example by paying someone to do tasks we dislike, can also increase well-being.

In the end, money seems to be a tool rather than a guarantee of happiness. It can protect us from hardship and give us opportunities, but relationships, health, purpose, and the way we use our resources are just as important.

=== b2-the-fight-against-malaria | The Fight Against Malaria | Health | B2
Malaria is one of the oldest and deadliest diseases known to humanity. It is caused by tiny parasites that are transmitted to people through the bites of infected mosquitoes. Symptoms include high fever, chills, and headaches, and without treatment, the disease can quickly become life-threatening. Despite enormous progress in recent decades, malaria still kills hundreds of thousands of people every year, most of them young children in Africa.

The parasites have a complex life cycle. When an infected mosquito bites a person, the parasites travel to the liver, where they multiply. They then enter the bloodstream and infect red blood cells. When another mosquito bites the infected person, it picks up the parasites, and the cycle begins again.

For centuries, people had little understanding of the disease. The name malaria comes from the Italian words for "bad air," because people believed it was caused by the unhealthy air near swamps. It was not until the late nineteenth century that scientists discovered the role of parasites and mosquitoes.

One of the simplest and most effective tools in the fight against malaria is the insecticide-treated bed net. Mosquitoes that carry malaria usually bite at night, so sleeping under a net provides strong protection. Millions of nets have been distributed in affected countries, and they have helped to save countless lives. Spraying the inside walls of homes with insecticide and providing fast diagnosis and treatment have also been important.

However, the battle is far from won. In some regions, mosquitoes have developed resistance to common insecticides, and parasites have become resistant to some drugs. Climate change may allow mosquitoes to spread into new areas.

In recent years, there has been a major breakthrough: the first vaccines against malaria. After decades of research, the World Health Organization recommended a malaria vaccine for children in two thousand twenty-one, followed by a second vaccine two years later. They are not perfect, but they can significantly reduce the number of severe cases and deaths. Scientists are also experimenting with other innovative approaches, including genetically modified mosquitoes that cannot transmit the parasite. Many experts now believe that eliminating malaria in our lifetime is a realistic goal.

=== b2-the-art-of-japanese-repair | The Beauty of Broken Things | Culture | B2
In most cultures, when a precious bowl or cup breaks, we either throw it away or try to repair it so that the damage is as invisible as possible. In Japan, however, there is a traditional art that takes a completely different approach. It is called kintsugi, which means "golden joinery," and it involves repairing broken pottery with lacquer mixed with powdered gold.

The result is striking. Instead of hiding the cracks, kintsugi highlights them, turning them into shining golden lines that run across the surface of the object. The repaired piece is often considered more beautiful and valuable than it was before it broke. Each piece becomes unique, because no two objects break in exactly the same way.

According to one popular story, kintsugi began in the fifteenth century, when a Japanese military ruler broke his favorite tea bowl. He sent it to China to be repaired, but it came back held together with ugly metal staples. Unhappy with the result, he asked Japanese craftsmen to find a more attractive method, and kintsugi was born. Whether or not this story is true, the technique became highly valued, and some collectors were even accused of breaking pottery deliberately so that it could be repaired with gold.

The process is slow and requires great skill. The lacquer used comes from the sap of a tree and must be applied in several thin layers, each of which needs to dry for days or even weeks. A single repair can take a month or more to complete.

Kintsugi is closely connected to Japanese philosophy. It reflects the idea of wabi-sabi, which finds beauty in imperfection, simplicity, and the passage of time. It also relates to the idea of mottainai, a sense of regret at waste.

In recent years, kintsugi has become popular around the world, not only as a craft but as a metaphor. Many people find in it a powerful message about life: that our scars and failures do not need to be hidden, and that the experiences that break us can become part of what makes us strong and unique. In a culture that often values perfection and replaces broken things without a second thought, kintsugi offers a different way of seeing damage.

=== b2-the-sleep-crisis | Are We Sleeping Less Than Ever? | Health | B2
Sleep experts generally recommend that adults get between seven and nine hours of sleep every night. Yet surveys in many countries suggest that a large proportion of people regularly sleep less than this. Some researchers have described insufficient sleep as a global public health problem, with serious consequences for our health, safety, and productivity.

Modern life presents many obstacles to good sleep. Long working hours, commuting, and the pressure to be constantly available make it hard for many people to get to bed early. Artificial light, especially the blue light emitted by phones, tablets, and computers, can interfere with the production of melatonin, a hormone that helps regulate our sleep cycle. Caffeine, alcohol, and irregular schedules can also disrupt sleep.

The effects of sleep deprivation go far beyond feeling tired. In the short term, lack of sleep impairs concentration, memory, and decision-making. It makes us more irritable and less able to cope with stress. Studies have shown that staying awake for around twenty hours can affect our performance as much as drinking alcohol. Drowsy driving is a major cause of road accidents.

In the long term, chronic lack of sleep has been linked to a wide range of health problems, including obesity, diabetes, heart disease, and depression. Sleep also appears to play a role in clearing waste products from the brain, and some research suggests that poor sleep over many years may increase the risk of dementia.

Teenagers are particularly affected. During adolescence, the body's internal clock shifts, so teenagers naturally tend to fall asleep and wake up later. Early school start times force many of them to wake up before their bodies are ready, leaving them chronically tired. Some school districts that have moved their start times later have reported improvements in attendance, grades, and mood.

Fortunately, there are many ways to improve sleep. Experts recommend going to bed and getting up at the same time every day, even on weekends. Keeping the bedroom dark, cool, and quiet, avoiding screens for an hour before bed, and limiting caffeine in the afternoon can all help. Perhaps most importantly, we need to stop seeing sleep as a waste of time and start treating it as an essential part of a healthy life.

=== b2-the-woman-who-mapped-the-ocean | Mapping the Ocean Floor | Science | B2
For most of human history, the bottom of the ocean was a complete mystery. Many people imagined it as a vast, flat, featureless plain covered in mud. In the nineteen fifties, an American geologist and cartographer named Marie Tharp proved them dramatically wrong, and in doing so, she helped to transform our understanding of the planet.

Tharp worked at a research institute at Columbia University in New York. At the time, women were not allowed to join research voyages, so she could not go to sea herself. Instead, her colleague Bruce Heezen collected data from ships that measured the depth of the ocean using sound waves. Tharp's job was to turn thousands of these measurements into maps.

It was painstaking work, done entirely by hand. But as she plotted the data from the Atlantic Ocean, Tharp noticed something extraordinary. Running down the middle of the ocean was a huge mountain range, and along its center was a deep valley, or rift. She concluded that the ocean floor was splitting apart along this line.

This idea supported a theory known as continental drift, which proposed that the continents slowly move across the surface of the Earth. At the time, most geologists rejected the theory. When Tharp showed her findings to Heezen, he dismissed them as "girl talk." It took him almost a year to accept that she was right.

Tharp and Heezen continued to map the ocean floor for many years. Their work revealed a global system of ridges and valleys running through all the world's oceans, like the seams on a baseball. In nineteen seventy-seven, they published a famous map of the entire ocean floor, painted by an Austrian artist, which showed underwater mountains, valleys, and plains in remarkable detail.

Their discoveries provided key evidence for the theory of plate tectonics, which is now the foundation of modern geology. It explains earthquakes, volcanoes, and the formation of mountains. For many years, Tharp received little recognition, and credit for the discovery often went to male scientists. Today, she is celebrated as one of the most important scientists of the twentieth century, a woman who mapped a hidden world she was never allowed to visit.
