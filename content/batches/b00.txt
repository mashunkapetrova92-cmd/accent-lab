=== unanswered-letters | The Unanswered Letters | Short Fiction
Nora had worked at the city archive for almost eleven years, and in all that time nothing had ever arrived without a label or a catalog number. The battered leather suitcase that appeared on her desk one wet Tuesday morning had neither. Its brass clasps were stiff with rust, and the lining smelled faintly of cedar and something sweeter, like dried lavender. Inside, tied together with a faded blue ribbon, lay several hundred letters, all addressed in the same elegant, slanting hand.

Her supervisor, a thoroughly practical man named Gerald, suggested she simply log the contents and move on. "Unclaimed correspondence," he said, barely glancing at it. "We have entire basements full of it." Nora nodded, but she was already reading the first envelope.

The letters were written by a woman who signed herself simply as Margaret, and they were addressed to someone she called her dearest Theodore. They spanned nearly two decades, beginning shortly after the war and ending, quite abruptly, in the middle of a sentence. What struck Nora most was not the romance, though there was plenty of it, but the remarkable precision of the details. Margaret described the weather, the price of bread, and the color of a neighbor's curtains, as though she were deliberately preserving a world she knew would vanish.

Over the following weeks, Nora found herself staying late, long after the reading room had emptied and the radiators had begun their familiar evening rattle. She transcribed each letter carefully, comparing street names with old municipal records and faded photographs. Gradually, a picture began to emerge. Margaret had lived above a bakery near the harbor, worked as a seamstress, and walked every Sunday to the end of the pier, where she claimed the light was particularly honest in the late afternoon. Theodore, it seemed, had been a merchant sailor whose ship rarely stayed in port for more than a few days. Her letters followed him across continents, waiting patiently in shipping offices from Lisbon to Singapore, often arriving months after they were written.

Yet not a single reply survived. Nora searched every box in the donation, every pocket of the suitcase, and even the torn lining, but found nothing written in any hand other than Margaret's. It was as if Theodore had simply listened, silently, across all those years. The breakthrough came from an unexpected source. An elderly volunteer named Walter, who had spent his entire career in the merchant navy, recognized the name of a vessel mentioned in one of the later letters and offered to make a few inquiries. Within two weeks, he returned with a photocopied page from a crew register. Theodore had indeed served aboard that ship, but the register recorded something Margaret could never have known when she wrote her final letter. He had been lost at sea the previous winter.

Nora read the entry three times before its meaning fully registered. For almost a year, Margaret had continued writing to a man who was no longer alive, describing her ordinary days to someone who would never read them. And yet the letters themselves had clearly been opened, read, and carefully refolded. Someone had kept them, ribbon and all, for more than half a century. The question was no longer who had written them, but who had cherished them enough to preserve them.

The donation form offered a single clue: a surname, a partially smudged address, and a signature that was almost illegible. It took Nora three evenings and an embarrassing number of phone calls to trace it to a small retirement home on the outskirts of town. The woman who met her in the visitors' lounge was frail but remarkably alert, with sharp gray eyes and a cardigan buttoned unevenly to the collar. When Nora mentioned the suitcase, the woman's hands, which had been folded neatly in her lap, went completely still.

Her name was Elizabeth, and she was Theodore's younger sister. After his death, the shipping company had forwarded his belongings to the family, and for months afterward, Margaret's letters had kept arriving at their door. "I couldn't bring myself to tell her," Elizabeth admitted quietly. "Every time I sat down to write, I imagined her reading it on that pier, and I simply couldn't. So I kept them. I read every single one, and I suppose that was my way of answering."

Nora asked whether she had ever tried to find Margaret later. Elizabeth shook her head. She had heard, years afterward, that Margaret had married, moved inland, and raised a family, and she had decided that some stories are kinder when they are allowed to remain unfinished. Before Nora left, Elizabeth asked for one thing: that the letters be kept together, exactly as they were, and that nobody ever separate them for the sake of a tidier catalog. Nora promised, and this time she meant it far more than any professional obligation required.

Back at the archive, she created a new entry with a description far longer than the regulations permitted. Gerald read it, raised an eyebrow, and said nothing. That evening, Nora tied the faded ribbon around the letters once more and placed the suitcase, with great care, on the highest shelf.
