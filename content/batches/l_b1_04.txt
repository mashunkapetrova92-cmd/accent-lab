=== b1-the-power-of-habits | How Habits Work | Psychology | B1
Every day, we do hundreds of things without really thinking about them. We brush our teeth, make coffee, check our phones, and take the same route to work. Researchers believe that around forty percent of our daily actions are habits, not conscious decisions.

Habits form because our brains like to save energy. When we repeat an action many times in the same situation, the brain starts to do it automatically. Scientists describe habits as a loop with three parts. First, there is a cue, something that triggers the habit, such as feeling bored or arriving home. Then there is the routine, the action itself, like eating a snack. Finally, there is the reward, the good feeling that makes the brain want to repeat the action.

Understanding this loop can help us change our habits. Experts say that it is very difficult to simply stop a bad habit. It is easier to keep the same cue and reward but change the routine. For example, if you always eat cookies when you feel tired in the afternoon, you could try going for a short walk instead. The cue, tiredness, is the same, and the reward, a short break and a bit of energy, is similar.

Building good habits works in a similar way. It helps to start very small. If you want to exercise more, begin with five minutes a day rather than an hour. Linking a new habit to an existing one also helps, for example doing a few stretches right after you brush your teeth in the morning.

Most importantly, be patient. Research suggests that it usually takes about two months for a new behavior to become automatic.

=== b1-the-wheelchair-explorer | Exploring the World on Wheels | Travel | B1
When Sarah was twenty-three, a car accident left her unable to walk. She had always loved traveling, and at first, she thought her adventures were over. Simple things like getting on a bus or entering a restaurant suddenly became major challenges. For more than a year, she hardly left her hometown.

Then a friend invited her on a trip to Spain. Sarah was nervous, but she decided to go. The trip was not easy. Some streets were too steep, some hotels said they were accessible but had steps at the entrance, and the airline damaged her wheelchair. But she also discovered that most problems had solutions, and that many people were happy to help.

That trip changed her life. Over the next ten years, Sarah visited more than forty countries. She has traveled through deserts, visited ancient temples, and even gone diving in the ocean with a specially trained instructor. She started writing a blog about her experiences, giving practical advice to other travelers with disabilities.

Her blog now has thousands of readers. She reviews hotels, museums, and tourist attractions, and she explains exactly how accessible they really are. She has also worked with tourism companies to help them improve their services for disabled visitors.

Sarah says the biggest barriers are often not physical but in people's minds. "People assume I can't do things," she says. "Sometimes I assumed it too. But almost everything is possible if you plan carefully and are willing to try." Her message to others is simple: don't let anybody, including yourself, decide what you can't do.

=== b1-the-color-of-food | How Color Changes Taste | Science | B1
Imagine drinking a glass of orange juice that is colored bright blue. Would it taste the same? Most people would say yes. But scientists have discovered that the color of our food has a surprisingly strong influence on how we think it tastes.

In one famous experiment, wine experts were given white wine that had been colored red with a flavorless dye. When they described it, they used words that are normally used for red wine, such as "berry" and "chocolate." Their eyes had tricked their sense of taste. Other experiments have shown that people think drinks taste sweeter when they are red or pink, and more sour when they are green or yellow.

The color of the plate also matters. In some studies, people ate less food when the color of the plate was very different from the color of the food. Desserts served on white plates were rated as sweeter and more delicious than the same desserts on black plates.

This happens because taste is not only about our tongue. When we eat, our brain combines information from all our senses, including sight, smell, and even sound. Before we taste anything, our brain has already made predictions based on how the food looks. If the food looks fresh and ripe, we expect it to taste good.

Food companies know this very well, and they spend a lot of money choosing the right colors for their products and packages. Chefs also think carefully about color when they present their dishes. So the next time you eat, remember that you are tasting with your eyes as well as your mouth.

=== b1-the-boy-and-the-orchestra | The Recycled Orchestra | Culture | B1
Cateura is a poor neighborhood in Paraguay that was built next to a huge landfill site. Many families there earn a living by collecting and selling recyclable materials from the trash. For the children who grow up there, opportunities are limited, and many leave school early to work.

In two thousand six, an environmental technician named Favio Chavez started working at the landfill. He was also a musician, and he decided to offer free music lessons to local children. But there was a problem. He only had a few instruments, and they were too expensive for the families to buy. A violin cost more than a house in the neighborhood.

Then a local trash picker called Nicolas Gomez had an idea. He started making instruments from the materials he found in the landfill. He made violins from old metal cans and pieces of wood, a cello from an oil can, and flutes from old water pipes. He even used forks and coins to make some parts. The instruments looked strange, but they sounded surprisingly good.

With these instruments, the children formed an orchestra. They practiced classical music, as well as popular songs. Their story was shared online and in a documentary film, and soon the Recycled Orchestra was invited to perform in concert halls around the world. They have played in Europe, Asia, and North America, and they even performed with famous rock bands.

For the young musicians, the orchestra has changed their lives. It has given them confidence, new skills, and the chance to travel. As their founder likes to say, "The world sends us garbage. We send back music."

=== b1-should-we-eat-insects | Should We Eat Insects? | Food | B1
For many people in Europe and North America, the idea of eating insects is disgusting. But for about two billion people around the world, insects are a normal part of their diet. In countries such as Thailand, Mexico, and Ghana, people eat fried crickets, roasted grasshoppers, and ant eggs as tasty snacks or meals.

Some experts believe that insects could play an important role in feeding the world in the future. The world's population is growing, and producing enough meat for everybody is becoming a serious problem. Raising cows, pigs, and chickens requires huge amounts of land, water, and food, and it produces a lot of greenhouse gases.

Insects are much more efficient. They need far less space and food than other animals to produce the same amount of protein. Crickets, for example, need about twelve times less food than cows to produce the same amount of protein. Insects also produce much fewer greenhouse gases. And they are very nutritious, full of protein, vitamins, and minerals.

So why don't more people eat them? The main reason is cultural. Many people simply find the idea unpleasant. To solve this problem, some companies are turning insects into powder and using it to make products like pasta, protein bars, and cookies. In this form, the insects can't be seen, and many people say they can't even taste the difference.

Insect farming is still a small industry, and there are questions about safety and how to produce insects on a large scale. But who knows? In twenty years, a cricket burger might be as normal as a beef burger.

=== b1-the-lonely-astronaut | Alone in Space | Science | B1
In nineteen sixty-nine, millions of people around the world watched on television as Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin became the first humans to walk on the Moon. But there was a third astronaut on the mission who never set foot on the Moon. His name was Michael Collins.

While Armstrong and Aldrin were on the surface, Collins stayed in the command module, flying in orbit around the Moon. His job was to keep the spacecraft ready to bring his two colleagues back. If something went wrong on the surface, he would have to return to Earth alone.

Every two hours, as the spacecraft traveled around the far side of the Moon, Collins lost all radio contact with Earth. For about forty-eight minutes each time, he was completely cut off from the rest of humanity. At those moments, he was further from other people than any human had ever been. Some newspapers later called him "the loneliest man in history."

But Collins said he didn't feel lonely at all. In his book, he wrote that he felt awareness, anticipation, and even joy. He enjoyed the silence, and he described the experience of seeing the stars without the light of the Earth as beautiful.

After the mission, many people asked him if he was disappointed that he didn't walk on the Moon. He always said no. He felt that he had been an important part of the team and was proud of what they had achieved together. Collins later became the director of the National Air and Space Museum in Washington.

=== b1-the-midnight-feast | The Midnight Feast | Short Fiction | B1
It was the last night of summer camp, and the twelve girls in Cabin Seven had been planning their midnight feast for a week. They had saved candy from the camp shop, hidden cookies under their beds, and someone's mother had sent a whole chocolate cake in the mail.

The rules were very clear: lights out at ten o'clock, and nobody was allowed to leave the cabins at night. The camp counselor, a strict young woman called Miss Turner, walked around the cabins every hour with a flashlight. But at midnight, when everything was quiet, the girls crept out of bed and gathered in the middle of the cabin floor.

They spread out a blanket and laid out all the food. They whispered and giggled as they shared the cake. Somebody started telling a ghost story, and just at the scariest moment, there was a loud knock on the door. Everybody screamed. The door opened slowly, and a bright light shone into the room. It was Miss Turner.

The girls froze. They were sure they were in terrible trouble. Miss Turner looked at the blanket, the cake, and the twelve frightened faces. Then, to everybody's surprise, she sat down on the floor. "Is there any cake left?" she asked. "When I was your age, I stayed in this very same cabin. We had a midnight feast on the last night too."

They talked and laughed until two o'clock in the morning. Many years later, the girls still remembered that night as the best night of camp, and Miss Turner as their favorite counselor.

=== b1-how-to-reduce-stress | Five Ways to Beat Stress | Health | B1
Stress is a normal part of life. A little stress can even be useful, because it gives us energy and helps us concentrate when we have a deadline or an important exam. But when stress continues for a long time, it can damage our health. It can cause headaches, sleep problems, and stomach problems, and it can make us more likely to get sick.

The first way to reduce stress is to move your body. Exercise is one of the most effective ways to fight stress. It doesn't have to be intense. A brisk walk, a bike ride, or some gentle yoga can help your body release tension and improve your mood.

Second, pay attention to your breathing. When we are stressed, we often breathe quickly and shallowly. Taking slow, deep breaths can calm the nervous system. One simple method is to breathe in for four seconds, hold your breath for four seconds, and breathe out for four seconds. Repeating this for a few minutes can make a big difference.

Third, talk to someone. Sharing your worries with a friend, a family member, or a professional can make problems feel smaller. Often, simply putting your feelings into words helps you see the situation more clearly.

Fourth, take a break from screens. Constant emails, news, and social media can make us feel overwhelmed. Try to have some time each day without your phone. And fifth, make time for things you enjoy, whether it is reading, cooking, listening to music, or spending time in nature. These moments of pleasure are not a luxury. They are essential for good health.

=== b1-the-island-of-cats | The Island of Cats | Places | B1
Off the coast of Japan, there is a small island called Aoshima. Fewer than ten people live there, and most of them are old. But the island is far from empty. It is home to well over a hundred cats, and it has become famous around the world as "Cat Island."

The cats were originally brought to the island many years ago to control the mice on the fishing boats. At that time, Aoshima had a busy fishing community of about nine hundred people. But over the years, young people left the island to find work in the cities, and the population fell sharply. Meanwhile, the cats, with no natural predators, kept on multiplying.

Today, the cats are everywhere. They sleep on walls, sit on boats, and wait near the harbor for the arrival of the small ferry that brings visitors from the mainland. As soon as tourists step off the boat, dozens of cats come running toward them, hoping for food.

The island has no shops, restaurants, or hotels, so visitors must bring their own food and water. There are also rules to protect the cats and the residents. Visitors are asked not to leave any food behind and to be quiet and respectful.

Some residents enjoy the attention, while others find the crowds of tourists tiring. In recent years, animal welfare groups have worked with the islanders to give the cats medical care and to stop the population from growing too large. For cat lovers, a day trip to Aoshima is like a dream come true.

=== b1-the-woman-who-counted | The Woman Who Calculated the Stars | History | B1
In nineteen sixty-two, the American astronaut John Glenn was preparing to become the first American to orbit the Earth. The mission was very dangerous, and the calculations for his flight had been made by new electronic computers. But Glenn didn't completely trust the machines. Before the flight, he made a special request: he wanted a woman called Katherine Johnson to check the numbers by hand.

Katherine Johnson was a brilliant mathematician. Born in West Virginia in nineteen eighteen, she showed an incredible talent for numbers from a very young age. She finished high school at fourteen and university at eighteen. But as an African American woman at that time, she faced many barriers. Schools and workplaces were segregated, and women were rarely given important jobs in science.

In nineteen fifty-three, she started working at the organization that later became NASA. She was one of a group of women who worked as "human computers," doing complex calculations by hand. Her skill quickly made her stand out. She calculated the paths of spacecraft, including the flight that took the first American into space.

When Glenn asked for her, Katherine spent days checking the computer's calculations. She confirmed that they were correct, and the mission was a success. Later, she also worked on the calculations for the missions that took astronauts to the Moon.

For many years, her work was not widely known. But in two thousand fifteen, she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom, one of the highest honors in the United States. A year later, a popular film called "Hidden Figures" told her story to the world.

=== b1-why-we-laugh | Why Do We Laugh? | Science | B1
Laughter is one of the most human things we do. Babies start laughing when they are just a few months old, long before they can speak. And people laugh in every culture in the world. But why do we laugh, and what is it for?

Most people think we laugh mainly because something is funny. But research shows that this is not quite true. In one study, scientists listened to people's conversations in public places and recorded when they laughed. They found that most laughter did not follow jokes at all. People laughed at ordinary comments, like "See you later" or "I know what you mean."

This suggests that laughter is mainly a social signal. It shows other people that we are friendly, that we agree with them, and that we feel comfortable with them. In fact, we are about thirty times more likely to laugh when we are with other people than when we are alone. Laughter is also contagious. When we hear other people laughing, we often start laughing too, even if we don't know why.

Laughter has benefits for our health as well. When we laugh, our body releases chemicals called endorphins, which make us feel good and can even reduce pain. Laughter can also reduce stress hormones and relax our muscles.

Interestingly, humans are not the only animals that laugh. Apes like chimpanzees make laughing sounds when they play and tickle each other. Scientists have even discovered that rats make tiny squeaking sounds, too high for humans to hear, when they are tickled. Laughter, it seems, is a very old part of our nature.

=== b1-the-kindness-of-strangers | Lost in Tokyo | Travel | B1
On my first trip to Japan, I made a mistake that I will never forget. On my second day in Tokyo, I decided to explore the city alone. I took a train to a district I had read about, walked around for a few hours, and then realized I had no idea where I was. My phone battery was dead, and I couldn't read any of the signs.

I started to panic. It was getting dark, and the streets were busy with people rushing home from work. I tried to ask a few people for directions, but they didn't speak English, and I didn't speak Japanese. I showed them the name of my hotel, which I had written on a piece of paper, but they just shook their heads politely.

Then an elderly man stopped and looked at my piece of paper. He didn't speak English either, but he smiled and made a sign for me to follow him. I was a little nervous, but I didn't have much choice. We walked to a train station, where he bought two tickets and got on the train with me.

We traveled for about twenty minutes. Then he led me out of the station and through several streets until we arrived in front of my hotel. I was so grateful that I wanted to give him some money or buy him dinner, but he refused. He simply bowed, smiled, and walked back toward the station.

I never learned his name. But whenever I see a tourist who looks lost in my own city, I remember that man, and I stop to help.

=== b1-the-zero-waste-family | Living Without Trash | Environment | B1
The average person in Europe produces around five hundred kilos of household waste every year. But some families have managed to reduce their waste to almost nothing. They follow a lifestyle known as "zero waste," and their entire year's trash can fit into a single small jar.

One of the most famous examples is Bea Johnson, a French woman living in California. In two thousand eight, she and her husband decided to change their lifestyle. They wanted to live more simply and protect the environment. Over time, they found ways to avoid almost all packaging and single-use items.

Their approach is based on five simple rules, in this order: refuse, reduce, reuse, recycle, and rot. First, they refuse things they don't need, such as free pens, plastic bags, and advertising leaflets. Then they reduce the number of things they own. They reuse items whenever possible, for example by taking their own cloth bags and glass jars to the shops. They recycle what they cannot refuse, reduce, or reuse, and they turn food scraps into compost for the garden.

The family says that living this way has many benefits. They save a lot of money, because they buy less and avoid packaged products. Their home is much less cluttered, and they spend less time cleaning and shopping.

Of course, it isn't easy for everybody. Not all towns have shops where you can buy food without packaging, and it requires time and planning. But even small changes, like carrying a reusable water bottle or saying no to plastic bags, can make a real difference.

=== b1-the-teacher-who-changed-everything | The Teacher Who Believed in Me | School | B1
At the age of fifteen, I was close to leaving school. My grades were terrible, I often skipped lessons, and most of my teachers had given up on me. At home, things were difficult. My parents had just separated, and nobody seemed to notice how unhappy I was.

Then a new English teacher arrived at our school. Her name was Ms. Rivera. On her first day, she asked us to write a short story about anything we wanted. I wrote about a boy who ran away to the sea. It was messy and full of mistakes, but I put everything I felt into it.

The next week, Ms. Rivera asked me to stay after class. I expected to be in trouble again. Instead, she handed me back my story covered in notes. "This is really good," she said. "You have a gift for writing. Have you ever thought about developing it?" Nobody had ever told me I was good at anything.

From that moment, things began to change. Ms. Rivera lent me books and encouraged me to enter a writing competition. She let me stay in her classroom at lunchtime to write when I didn't want to be around other people. Slowly, I started attending more lessons, and my grades improved in other subjects too.

Today, I work as a journalist, and I have written two books. A few years ago, I went back to my old school to give a talk. Ms. Rivera was in the audience. When I thanked her publicly, she cried. One person's belief can change a whole life.

=== b1-the-sharing-economy | The Sharing Economy | Business | B1
In the past, if you wanted to stay in a city for a few days, you would book a hotel. If you needed to get across town, you would take a taxi. Today, many people use apps that connect them directly with ordinary people who can offer these services. This new way of doing business is often called the sharing economy.

The idea is simple. Many people own things that they don't use all the time, such as a spare room, a car, or tools. Through online platforms, they can rent these things to others and earn some extra money. Some apps let people rent out their homes to travelers, while others allow drivers to use their own cars as taxis. There are even apps for sharing parking spaces, clothes, and dogs.

For customers, the sharing economy often offers lower prices and more choice. Travelers can stay in local neighborhoods instead of tourist areas and experience a city more like a resident. For providers, it can be a flexible way to earn money and make better use of their possessions.

However, the sharing economy has also caused problems. In some popular cities, so many apartments are rented to tourists that there are fewer homes available for local people, and rents have gone up. Taxi drivers have protested, saying that app drivers don't have to follow the same rules. There are also concerns about workers' rights, because many app workers are not employees and don't receive benefits like sick pay or vacations.

Many cities are now introducing new laws to control the sharing economy. The challenge is to keep its benefits while protecting the people affected by it.

=== b1-the-power-of-reading | Why Reading Fiction Is Good for You | Culture | B1
In a world full of television, video games, and social media, reading novels might seem old-fashioned. But scientists are discovering that reading fiction has surprising benefits for our brains and our relationships with other people.

One of the most interesting findings is that reading fiction may help us understand other people better. When we read a novel, we see the world through the eyes of the characters. We learn about their thoughts, feelings, and motivations. Some studies suggest that people who read a lot of fiction are better at understanding what others are thinking and feeling. This ability is known as empathy.

Reading is also good exercise for the brain. When we read, we have to concentrate, imagine scenes and characters, and remember complicated plots. Brain scans have shown that reading a story activates many different parts of the brain, including areas connected with movement and the senses. When we read about a character running, the parts of our brain that control running become more active.

Reading can also reduce stress. In one study, just six minutes of reading reduced stress levels more than listening to music or going for a walk. Losing ourselves in a good book allows us to escape our worries for a while.

Finally, regular reading may help to protect the brain as we get older. Studies suggest that people who read and do other mentally challenging activities throughout their lives may keep their memory sharper in old age. So the next time someone tells you that reading novels is a waste of time, you can tell them it is good for your health.

=== b1-the-surprise-inheritance | The Unexpected Gift | Short Fiction | B1

=== b1-the-tallest-buildings | Racing to the Sky | Technology | B1
For most of human history, the tallest buildings in the world were religious buildings, such as churches, temples, and pyramids. The Great Pyramid of Giza in Egypt held the record for nearly four thousand years. But in the late nineteenth century, a new type of building began to change city skylines forever: the skyscraper.

Two inventions made skyscrapers possible. The first was the use of steel frames, which were strong enough to support very tall buildings without needing extremely thick walls. The second was the safe elevator. Before this, nobody wanted to climb more than a few floors of stairs. The first skyscrapers appeared in Chicago and New York in the eighteen eighties.

In the early twentieth century, there was a competition between cities and companies to build the tallest building. In New York, the Chrysler Building and the Empire State Building were completed within a year of each other. The Empire State Building was the world's tallest building for almost forty years.

Today, the tallest building in the world is the Burj Khalifa in Dubai, which is more than eight hundred meters tall. Building it required solutions to many new problems. At that height, the wind is extremely strong, so the building was designed in a special shape to reduce its effects. Its elevators are among the fastest in the world.

Even taller buildings are being planned. But some architects question whether we need them. Very tall buildings are expensive to build and use a lot of energy. Some cities prefer to grow in other ways, with lower buildings, green spaces, and good public transportation.

=== b1-the-man-who-walked-around-the-world | Walking Around the World | Travel | B1
In nineteen seventy, a young American called Dave Kunst set off from his home in Minnesota with his brother John and a mule named Willie Makeit. Their plan was to walk all the way around the world. Nobody had ever done it before, and many people thought it was impossible.

The brothers walked across the United States, then flew across the Atlantic and continued walking through Europe and Asia. Along the way, they collected money for a children's charity. Everywhere they went, people were curious about the two men and their mule. They were often invited into people's homes for meals and given a place to sleep.

But the journey also had terrible moments. In Afghanistan, the brothers were attacked by robbers. John was killed, and Dave was seriously injured. It took Dave four months to recover. Many people expected him to give up and go home. Instead, he decided to continue the walk, both for himself and in memory of his brother.

He continued with another brother, Pete, and later walked alone. He crossed India and then flew to Australia, where he walked across the vast, hot desert. During this part of the journey, he met a teacher called Jenny, who drove a car behind him with supplies for many weeks. They later got married.

In October nineteen seventy-four, more than four years after he had left, Dave walked back into his hometown. He had walked about twenty-three thousand kilometers and worn out twenty-one pairs of shoes. He was the first person ever confirmed to have walked around the world.

=== b1-the-night-sky | Why We Are Losing the Night Sky | Environment | B1
For most of human history, people could look up on a clear night and see thousands of stars and the bright band of the Milky Way stretching across the sky. Today, most people who live in cities have never seen it. The reason is light pollution: the artificial light from streetlights, buildings, cars, and advertisements that brightens the night sky.

Scientists estimate that more than a third of the world's population can no longer see the Milky Way from where they live. In Europe and North America, the figure is even higher. And the problem is getting worse. A recent study found that the night sky is getting brighter by almost ten percent every year in some places.

Light pollution is not only a problem for people who enjoy looking at the stars. It also affects wildlife. Many animals use the natural light of the moon and stars to find their way. Baby sea turtles, for example, are supposed to move toward the light of the sea after they hatch, but bright lights from hotels and roads can confuse them and lead them in the wrong direction. Birds that migrate at night can also become lost, and many insects die around streetlights.

Artificial light may affect our health as well. Our bodies need darkness to produce a hormone that helps us sleep, and too much light at night can disturb our natural rhythms.

The good news is that light pollution is easy to reduce. Using lights only when they are needed, pointing them downward, and choosing warmer colors can make a big difference. Some places have become official "dark sky reserves," where the night sky is protected for everybody to enjoy.
