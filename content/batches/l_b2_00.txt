=== b2-the-attention-economy | The Battle for Your Attention | Technology | B2
Every time you open a social media app, you are entering a competition you probably didn't agree to join. On one side is you, with a limited amount of time and attention. On the other side are teams of highly skilled engineers, designers, and psychologists whose job is to keep you scrolling for as long as possible. This is what some critics call the attention economy.

The business model is simple. Most social media platforms are free to use, but they make money by selling advertising. The more time users spend on the platform, the more ads they see, and the more money the company earns. As a result, these apps are carefully designed to capture and hold our attention.

Many of the techniques they use are based on research into human behavior. Infinite scrolling, for example, removes natural stopping points, so there is never a moment when you reach the end and decide to put your phone down. Notifications, often shown in red, create a sense of urgency that is hard to ignore. And the unpredictable rewards of social media, such as not knowing how many likes a post will receive, work in a similar way to a slot machine, encouraging us to check again and again.

Some former employees of large technology companies have become outspoken critics of these designs. They argue that the constant competition for attention is damaging our ability to concentrate, increasing anxiety, particularly among teenagers, and making it harder for people to have meaningful conversations.

Technology companies, on the other hand, point out that their products also bring enormous benefits. They help people stay in touch with friends and family, discover new ideas, and build communities around shared interests. Several platforms have also introduced features that allow users to set time limits or see how long they have spent on the app.

Ultimately, many experts believe that individuals need to become more aware of how these technologies work. Simple steps, such as turning off non-essential notifications, keeping phones out of the bedroom, or moving distracting apps off the home screen, can help us take back some control. After all, if a product is free, it is worth asking what we are really paying with.

=== b2-the-placebo-effect | The Power of Belief | Science | B2
Imagine taking a pill for a headache and feeling better within twenty minutes, only to discover later that the pill contained nothing but sugar. This is not a trick or a coincidence. It is an example of one of the most fascinating phenomena in medicine: the placebo effect.

A placebo is a treatment that has no active ingredient, such as a sugar pill or an injection of salt water. Yet when patients believe they are receiving real medicine, a significant number of them report genuine improvements in their symptoms. The effect has been observed for a wide range of conditions, including pain, depression, anxiety, and sleep problems.

For a long time, scientists assumed that the placebo effect was simply imaginary, and that patients only thought they felt better. However, modern brain scans have revealed that something real is happening in the body. When people expect pain relief, their brains release natural painkillers called endorphins. In other words, our expectations can trigger physical changes.

Remarkably, the details of a treatment can influence how well a placebo works. Studies have found that two pills tend to be more effective than one, that injections often work better than pills, and that expensive-looking medicine can produce stronger effects than cheap medicine. Even the color of a pill can make a difference.

Perhaps most surprisingly, placebos can sometimes work even when patients know they are taking one. In several experiments, patients with long-term conditions were given pills that were clearly labeled as placebos. Many still reported feeling better. Researchers think that the ritual of taking medicine, and the care and attention of the doctor, may play an important role.

The placebo effect also has a darker twin, known as the nocebo effect. When people expect a treatment to cause side effects, they are more likely to experience them, even if the treatment is harmless. This has led some doctors to think carefully about how they describe risks to their patients. The power of belief, it seems, can work in both directions.

=== b2-the-gig-economy | Living in the Gig Economy | Work | B2
For previous generations, the ideal career often meant finding a stable job with one company and staying there for decades. Today, a growing number of people are choosing, or being forced into, a very different way of working. They work in the so-called gig economy, taking on short-term jobs, or "gigs," rather than having a permanent position.

Gig workers include food delivery riders, app-based taxi drivers, and freelance designers, writers, and programmers who find clients through online platforms. Some do gig work as their main source of income, while others use it to earn extra money alongside a regular job.

Supporters of the gig economy emphasize the flexibility it offers. Workers can often choose when, where, and how much they work. This can be ideal for students, parents with young children, or people who want to pursue other interests, such as music or art. For some, being their own boss is a source of great satisfaction.

However, this flexibility comes at a price. Gig workers are usually classified as self-employed rather than employees, which means they are not entitled to many of the protections that traditional workers take for granted. They may not receive sick pay, paid vacation, or a pension, and they often have to pay for their own equipment and insurance. Their income can also be extremely unpredictable, varying significantly from week to week.

Critics argue that some companies use the gig model to reduce costs by shifting risk onto workers. Delivery riders, for example, may be paid per delivery and earn very little on quiet days, even though they have spent hours waiting for orders. In several countries, workers have taken legal action, arguing that they should be recognized as employees.

Governments are now trying to find a balance. Some have introduced new laws guaranteeing gig workers a minimum wage or basic protections, while trying not to destroy the flexibility that many workers value. As technology continues to change the world of work, the question of how to protect people in this new economy is likely to become even more important.

=== b2-the-lost-city | The Discovery of Machu Picchu | History | B2
High in the Andes mountains of Peru, at an altitude of almost two thousand five hundred meters, lie the ruins of an extraordinary city. Machu Picchu, with its stone temples, terraces, and houses perched on a narrow ridge between two mountains, is one of the most famous archaeological sites in the world. Every year, more than a million tourists travel there to see it.

The city was built in the fifteenth century by the Inca, who ruled a vast empire stretching along the western side of South America. Historians believe it was probably a royal estate for the emperor Pachacuti, a place where he and his family could relax, perform religious ceremonies, and escape the heat of the lowlands. Its builders were extraordinarily skilled. They cut huge stones so precisely that they fit together without any mortar, and many walls are so tight that not even a knife blade can be inserted between the stones.

Remarkably, Machu Picchu was abandoned only about a hundred years after it was built, possibly because of the arrival of the Spanish and the diseases they brought. Because of its remote location, the Spanish never found it, which is one of the reasons why it is so well preserved today.

The site became internationally famous in nineteen eleven, when an American explorer called Hiram Bingham was led there by local farmers. He published articles and photographs that amazed the world, and he is often described as the man who discovered Machu Picchu. However, this description is misleading. Local people had always known about the ruins, and some families were actually living and farming there when Bingham arrived.

Bingham's expeditions also removed thousands of objects from the site and took them to a university in the United States. For almost a century, Peru demanded their return, and most of them were finally sent back in the two thousand tens.

Today, Machu Picchu faces a different challenge: its own popularity. The huge number of visitors puts pressure on the ancient stones and the fragile mountain environment. The Peruvian government now limits the number of daily visitors and requires them to follow fixed routes, in the hope of preserving the site for future generations.

=== b2-the-inheritance | The Clockmaker's Daughter | Short Fiction | B2
Nobody in the village was surprised when old Thomas Webb left his house to his daughter. What surprised them was that he left his workshop, and the hundreds of clocks inside it, to a boy called Sam, the fourteen-year-old son of the family next door.

Sam had first wandered into the workshop when he was eight, drawn by the ticking that he could hear through the wall. Thomas, who rarely spoke to anyone, had looked at him over his glasses and asked, "Do you want to see how it works?" From that day on, Sam had spent almost every afternoon there, watching the old man's hands as he opened clocks, cleaned their tiny parts, and brought them back to life.

Thomas's daughter, Clara, lived in the city and had not visited for years. She arrived for the funeral wearing an expensive coat and an expression of polite impatience. When the lawyer read the will, she laughed in disbelief. "The clocks are worth a fortune," she said. "Some of them are two hundred years old. He can't seriously have given them to a child."

That evening, Clara knocked on Sam's door. She offered him a large sum of money for the workshop, more money than his family had ever seen. His parents looked at each other. Sam was silent for a long time. Then he asked her a question. "Do you know why he loved them?"

Clara didn't answer. So Sam took her to the workshop and showed her. He opened a small silver clock on the bench and pointed to a name engraved inside: Clara. "He made this for you when you were born," he said. "He told me he fixed it every year on your birthday. He hoped you'd come back for it one day."

Clara stood in the workshop for a long time. In the end, she didn't buy anything. She took the silver clock home with her, and she began visiting the village again. And every Saturday, she sat in the workshop while Sam worked, asking him to tell her stories about the father she had never really known.

=== b2-the-myth-of-multitasking | The Myth of Multitasking | Psychology | B2
Many of us are proud of our ability to multitask. We answer emails during meetings, listen to podcasts while we work, and check our phones while talking to friends. In job interviews, people often describe themselves as excellent multitaskers, as if it were a valuable skill. However, research in psychology suggests that true multitasking is largely a myth.

When we think we are doing two things at once, our brain is usually switching rapidly between them. Each time we switch, there is a small cost. The brain needs time to stop focusing on one task and adjust to the other. These costs may only last a fraction of a second, but they add up. Some researchers estimate that constant task switching can reduce our productivity by as much as forty percent.

Switching also makes us more likely to make mistakes. When our attention is divided, we process information less deeply and are more likely to miss important details. This can have serious consequences. Using a phone while driving, for example, significantly increases the risk of an accident, even when drivers use hands-free devices. The problem is not where our hands are, but where our attention is.

Interestingly, studies have found that people who multitask the most are often the worst at it. In one experiment, heavy media multitaskers performed worse than light multitaskers on tests of attention and memory. They were more easily distracted by irrelevant information and found it harder to switch between tasks efficiently.

There are some exceptions. We can combine an automatic activity, like walking, with a thinking task, like having a conversation, without much difficulty. Problems arise when both tasks require conscious attention.

So what is the alternative? Many productivity experts recommend a method called single-tasking: focusing on one task at a time for a set period, then taking a short break. Closing unnecessary browser tabs, turning off notifications, and setting specific times to check emails can all help. It might feel slower at first, but most people find that they get more done, and with less stress.

=== b2-the-blue-whale | The Largest Animal That Ever Lived | Animals | B2
The blue whale is not only the largest animal alive today. It is the largest animal that has ever lived on Earth, bigger than any dinosaur. An adult can grow to around thirty meters long and weigh as much as a hundred and fifty tons. Its heart alone is roughly the size of a small car, and its tongue can weigh as much as an elephant.

Despite its enormous size, the blue whale feeds almost entirely on krill, tiny shrimp-like creatures only a few centimeters long. Instead of teeth, it has hundreds of plates made of a material called baleen, which hang from its upper jaw like the bristles of a giant comb. To feed, the whale takes in a huge mouthful of water and krill, then pushes the water out through the baleen, trapping the krill inside. During the feeding season, a single blue whale can eat several tons of krill every day.

Blue whales are also among the loudest animals on the planet. They communicate using low-frequency sounds that can travel hundreds of kilometers through the ocean. Many of these sounds are too low for human ears to hear. Scientists are still trying to understand exactly what the whales are communicating, but the sounds may help them find each other across vast distances.

In the twentieth century, blue whales came close to extinction. Modern whaling ships with explosive harpoons made it possible to hunt them in large numbers, and hundreds of thousands were killed for their oil. By the time hunting was banned in the nineteen sixties, the population had fallen by more than ninety percent.

Since then, numbers have slowly begun to recover, but blue whales still face serious threats. They are sometimes hit by large ships, and they can become entangled in fishing equipment. Climate change may also affect the availability of krill, their only food source. Noise from shipping can interfere with their communication.

Protecting these gentle giants requires international cooperation, because they travel across entire oceans. Some countries have changed shipping routes or reduced ship speeds in areas where whales feed, and early results suggest these measures can save lives.

=== b2-should-university-be-free | Should University Be Free? | Education | B2
In some countries, university education is free or almost free for students. In others, students pay high tuition fees and often graduate with debts that take decades to repay. The question of who should pay for higher education is one of the most hotly debated issues in education policy.

Supporters of free university education argue that it is a matter of fairness. If students have to pay high fees, young people from poorer families may decide not to go to university at all, even if they are talented and hardworking. Free education, they say, gives everybody the same opportunities, regardless of their background. It also prevents graduates from starting their working lives burdened by heavy debt, which can affect their decisions about careers, housing, and starting a family.

Another argument is that society as a whole benefits from having more highly educated people. Graduates tend to earn more and therefore pay more tax. They are also more likely to contribute to research, innovation, and public services such as healthcare and education. From this perspective, investing in universities is an investment in the future of the whole country.

Opponents, however, point out that nothing is really free. If students do not pay, the cost must be covered by taxpayers, including people who have never been to university and may earn much less than graduates. They argue that it is unfair to ask them to fund the education of people who will benefit personally from higher salaries.

Critics also claim that free tuition does not necessarily help the poorest students. In some countries with free universities, students from wealthy families are still much more likely to attend, because they have had better schooling and more support at home. Some argue that the money would be better spent on improving schools or providing grants for living costs.

Many countries have tried to find a middle way. In some systems, students pay nothing at the start, but repay part of the cost later, and only if their income rises above a certain level. Whether this is the best solution remains a matter of debate, but the question is unlikely to disappear any time soon.

=== b2-the-history-of-money | From Shells to Smartphones | Business | B2
Today, many of us rarely touch physical money. We pay for coffee with our phones, transfer money with a few taps, and shop online with digital cards. But the idea of money is thousands of years old, and it has taken many surprising forms throughout history.

Before money existed, people traded goods directly, a system known as barter. A farmer might exchange a sack of grain for a pair of shoes. The problem with barter is that it only works when both people want what the other has. If the shoemaker doesn't need grain, the farmer is out of luck. Money solved this problem by acting as something that everybody would accept in exchange for goods.

Early forms of money varied enormously. In parts of Africa and Asia, people used cowrie shells, which were small, durable, and hard to fake. Other societies used salt, cattle, or large stone wheels. On the Pacific island of Yap, some stone coins were so heavy that they were never moved. Instead, everybody simply remembered who owned them.

The first metal coins appeared around two thousand six hundred years ago in Lydia, in what is now Turkey. Coins were convenient because their value was guaranteed by a ruler, and they were easy to count and carry. Paper money was first used in China around a thousand years ago. Merchants found it much easier to carry paper notes than heavy bags of coins.

For a long time, paper money represented a fixed amount of gold or silver stored by the government. Today, however, most money is not backed by any physical object. Its value depends on trust: we accept it because we believe that other people will accept it too.

In recent decades, money has become increasingly digital. In some countries, cash is now used for only a small percentage of payments. Cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin have also emerged, promising money that is not controlled by any government or bank. Whether they will become the future of money or remain a risky experiment is still unclear. What is certain is that the story of money is far from over.

=== b2-the-power-of-introverts | The Quiet Strength of Introverts | Psychology | B2
In many modern societies, the ideal personality seems to be outgoing, talkative, and confident. Schools encourage group work, offices are designed as open-plan spaces, and people who speak up in meetings are often seen as natural leaders. But what about the people who prefer quiet, who think before they speak, and who feel drained by too much social activity? These are introverts, and they make up perhaps a third to a half of the population.

It is important to understand that introversion is not the same as shyness. Shy people feel anxious in social situations and fear being judged. Introverts may be perfectly confident and socially skilled, but they find large social gatherings tiring and need time alone to recharge. Extroverts, by contrast, tend to gain energy from being around other people.

Psychologists believe these differences may be partly related to how our brains respond to stimulation. Introverts seem to be more sensitive to noise, light, and activity, so they prefer calmer environments. A loud party that energizes an extrovert may quickly overwhelm an introvert.

Introverts often have particular strengths. They tend to be good listeners and careful thinkers. Many are comfortable working alone for long periods, which can be ideal for tasks requiring deep concentration, such as writing, research, or programming. Some of history's greatest scientists, artists, and writers have described themselves as introverts.

Research has also challenged the assumption that extroverts always make better leaders. In one study, introverted leaders were more successful than extroverts when they led teams of proactive employees. Because they listened carefully and were open to suggestions, they made better use of their team's ideas.

None of this means that introversion is better than extroversion. Both have advantages, and most people are somewhere in between. But many experts believe that schools and workplaces could benefit from recognizing different personality types. Offering quiet spaces, allowing people to prepare before meetings, and valuing thoughtful contributions as well as loud ones can help everybody perform at their best.

=== b2-a-city-built-for-cyclists | How Copenhagen Learned to Cycle | Environment | B2
Every morning in Copenhagen, the capital of Denmark, tens of thousands of people cycle to work and school. They ride in wide, separate bike lanes, wait at traffic lights designed for bicycles, and cross bridges built only for cyclists and pedestrians. Today, more people in the city travel to work or school by bike than by car. But Copenhagen was not always a paradise for cyclists.

In the nineteen fifties and sixties, as in many European cities, cars were taking over. Streets were widened, squares were turned into parking lots, and cycling declined sharply. Then, in the nineteen seventies, two events changed the direction of the city. The first was an oil crisis, which caused fuel prices to rise dramatically. The second was growing public concern about traffic accidents, particularly those involving children. Thousands of people took part in demonstrations demanding safer streets.

In response, the city began investing heavily in cycling infrastructure. It built a network of bike lanes that are physically separated from car traffic, often by a curb. Traffic lights were adjusted so that cyclists get a green light a few seconds before cars, making them more visible at junctions. On some main routes, the lights are timed so that a cyclist riding at around twenty kilometers an hour can pass through a series of green lights without stopping.

The city also made cycling convenient in many smaller ways. There are footrests at traffic lights so that cyclists don't have to put their feet on the ground, special trash cans angled for cyclists to use while riding, and plenty of parking spaces for bikes at stations.

The benefits have been considerable. Cycling reduces traffic, pollution, and noise, and it keeps people healthy. Studies suggest that the health benefits alone save the city a significant amount of money every year.

For Copenhagen residents, cycling is not about being environmentally friendly or fit. It is simply the fastest and easiest way to get around. And that, according to city planners, is the real secret: if you make cycling the most convenient option, people will choose it.

=== b2-artificial-intelligence-and-art | Can a Machine Be an Artist? | Technology | B2
In two thousand eighteen, a portrait of a man in a dark coat was sold at a famous auction house in New York for more than four hundred thousand dollars. The painting was blurry and unfinished-looking, and in the bottom corner, where an artist's signature would normally be, there was a mathematical formula. The portrait had been created not by a human, but by a computer program.

Since then, artificial intelligence has become far more powerful. Today, anyone can type a short description into an AI tool, such as "a cat reading a newspaper in the style of a Renaissance painting," and receive a detailed, often impressive image in seconds. Similar tools can compose music, write poetry, and even create short films.

These developments have provoked intense debate. Some people are excited by the possibilities. They see AI as a new kind of creative tool, much like the camera or the computer, that can help people express their ideas. Artists who use AI often describe a kind of collaboration, in which they guide the machine, select the best results, and refine them.

Others are deeply concerned. Many professional illustrators, designers, and musicians fear that AI could take away their jobs, since companies may prefer to use cheap and fast machines rather than pay human artists. There are also serious questions about copyright. AI systems learn by analyzing millions of existing images and texts, many of them created by human artists who were never asked for permission or paid. Some artists have taken legal action, arguing that their work has been used without consent.

Beyond these practical concerns lies a more philosophical question: can a machine really be creative? Critics argue that art is about human experience, emotion, and intention. A machine, they say, has no feelings and nothing to express. It can only combine patterns it has seen before. Supporters reply that human artists also learn by studying the work of others, and that the emotional response of the audience is what matters most.

There may be no simple answer. What is clear is that AI is changing the way art is made and how we think about creativity. How society chooses to respond will shape the future of art for generations.

=== b2-the-lady-with-the-lamp | The Lady with the Lamp | History | B2
In eighteen fifty-four, Britain, France, and the Ottoman Empire were at war with Russia in the Crimean War. Reports soon reached London about the terrible conditions in British military hospitals. Wounded soldiers were dying in enormous numbers, not only from their injuries but from diseases such as cholera and typhus. In response, the government sent a team of nurses to the main British hospital in Scutari, in what is now Istanbul. Their leader was a thirty-four-year-old woman named Florence Nightingale.

Nightingale came from a wealthy family, and her parents had strongly opposed her desire to become a nurse. At the time, nursing was not considered a respectable profession for an educated woman. But she was determined, and she had studied nursing in Germany and France.

What she found at Scutari shocked her. The hospital was overcrowded and filthy. There were not enough beds, blankets, or clean water, and the drains beneath the building were blocked. Nightingale and her nurses set to work cleaning the wards, washing linen, and organizing proper meals. She wrote letters to newspapers and politicians demanding better supplies.

At night, she walked through the wards carrying a lamp to check on the patients. The soldiers began to call her "the Lady with the Lamp," and newspaper reports turned her into a national hero.

However, Nightingale's most lasting contribution was perhaps less romantic. She was a talented mathematician, and after the war, she collected and analyzed detailed statistics about the causes of death in military hospitals. She presented her findings in innovative, colorful diagrams that clearly showed that most soldiers had died from preventable diseases, not from their wounds. Her work convinced the government to improve sanitation in hospitals and the army.

In eighteen sixty, she founded a school of nursing in London, which helped to turn nursing into a respected, professional career. Today, her birthday, the twelfth of May, is celebrated around the world as International Nurses Day.

=== b2-microplastics | The Invisible Pollution | Environment | B2
When we think about plastic pollution, we usually picture bottles floating in the ocean or bags caught in trees. But some of the most worrying plastic pollution is almost invisible. Microplastics, tiny pieces of plastic smaller than five millimeters, have now been found almost everywhere scientists have looked, from the deepest ocean trenches to the snow on top of mountains.

Microplastics come from many different sources. Some are manufactured to be small, such as the tiny beads that were once commonly added to face washes and toothpastes. Many more are created when larger pieces of plastic break down over time due to sunlight, wind, and waves. Synthetic clothes, made from materials such as polyester and nylon, release thousands of tiny fibers every time they are washed. Car tires shed small particles as they wear down on the road.

Because they are so small, microplastics are easily carried by wind and water. They have been found in rivers, lakes, soil, and even in the air we breathe. They have also entered the food chain. Small sea creatures eat them, and these creatures are eaten by fish, which may end up on our plates. Microplastics have been detected in seafood, salt, honey, and bottled water.

Perhaps most alarmingly, scientists have now found microplastics inside the human body, including in blood, lungs, and even the placenta. What this means for our health is not yet clear. Some laboratory studies suggest that microplastics could cause inflammation or carry harmful chemicals into the body, but much more research is needed to understand the risks.

Dealing with microplastics is extremely difficult, because once they are in the environment, they are almost impossible to remove. That is why many experts believe the focus must be on prevention. Some countries have banned microbeads in cosmetics. Engineers are developing filters for washing machines that can catch fibers before they enter the water system.

Individuals can help too, by reducing their use of single-use plastics, choosing natural fabrics when possible, and washing synthetic clothes less often and at lower temperatures. The problem is huge, but every reduction in the amount of plastic we produce makes a difference.

=== b2-the-science-of-procrastination | Why We Put Things Off | Psychology | B2
You have an important task to complete, perhaps an essay, a report, or your tax return. You know it needs to be done, and you know you will feel better once it is finished. Yet instead of starting, you find yourself tidying your desk, checking your phone, or watching just one more video. Sound familiar? You are not alone. Studies suggest that around a fifth of adults are chronic procrastinators, and almost everybody procrastinates from time to time.

For a long time, procrastination was seen as a problem of laziness or poor time management. But psychologists now believe it is mainly a problem of emotion regulation. We procrastinate not because we are lazy, but because the task makes us feel bad. It might be boring, difficult, or confusing, or it might make us anxious about failing. Putting it off gives us immediate relief from these negative feelings.

The problem is that this relief is only temporary. As the deadline gets closer, the stress returns, often stronger than before. Many procrastinators also feel guilty and ashamed about delaying, which makes the task even more unpleasant and increases the temptation to avoid it again. It becomes a vicious circle.

Part of the explanation lies in how our brains think about the future. Research shows that we tend to think of our future selves almost as strangers. When we put off a task, we are essentially passing the problem to someone else, a future version of us who we imagine will have more time, energy, and motivation.

Fortunately, there are strategies that can help. One is to break large tasks into small, manageable steps, so that starting feels less overwhelming. Another is the "five-minute rule": commit to working on a task for just five minutes. Often, the hardest part is beginning, and once we have started, we continue. Removing distractions, such as putting your phone in another room, can also make a big difference.

Perhaps surprisingly, one of the most effective strategies is self-compassion. Studies have found that students who forgave themselves for procrastinating before one exam were less likely to procrastinate before the next. Being kind to ourselves, it seems, is more motivating than being harsh.

=== b2-the-story-of-the-olympics | The Ancient and Modern Olympics | History | B2
Every four years, thousands of athletes from around the world gather to compete in the Olympic Games, watched by billions of people on television. The modern Games are a huge global event, but their origins lie in a small sanctuary in ancient Greece almost three thousand years ago.

According to tradition, the first Olympic Games took place in seven hundred and seventy-six before Christ at Olympia, a religious site dedicated to the god Zeus. At first, there was only one event, a running race of about two hundred meters. Over time, more events were added, including wrestling, boxing, chariot racing, and the pentathlon, which combined running, jumping, wrestling, and throwing the discus and javelin.

The ancient Games were very different from today's. Only free Greek men were allowed to compete, and athletes competed naked. Married women were not even allowed to watch. Winners did not receive gold medals but crowns made of olive leaves, although they often received money, free meals, and great honor when they returned home. During the Games, a sacred truce was declared so that athletes and spectators could travel safely to Olympia.

The ancient Olympics continued for more than a thousand years until they were banned at the end of the fourth century after Christ by a Roman emperor who wanted to end pagan festivals.

The idea was revived in the late nineteenth century by a French educator named Pierre de Coubertin. He believed that sport could promote peace and understanding between nations. The first modern Olympic Games were held in Athens in eighteen ninety-six, with athletes from fourteen countries. Women competed for the first time four years later in Paris.

Since then, the Games have grown enormously and have reflected the history of the world around them. They were canceled during both world wars and have been affected by political boycotts. They have also faced problems with doping and the enormous costs of hosting. Yet for many people, the Olympics still represent Coubertin's original dream: a moment when the whole world comes together to celebrate human achievement.

=== b2-the-rewilding-movement | Bringing Nature Back | Environment | B2
In nineteen ninety-five, a small group of gray wolves was released into Yellowstone National Park in the United States. Wolves had been absent from the park for about seventy years, after they were hunted and killed to protect farm animals. Their return led to some remarkable changes, and it became one of the most famous examples of what is now known as rewilding.

Without wolves, the number of elk in Yellowstone had increased dramatically. The elk grazed heavily on young trees and plants, especially along rivers. When wolves returned, they hunted the elk and changed their behavior. Elk began avoiding open valleys where they could easily be attacked. As a result, trees such as willows and aspens began to grow back in some areas. This provided food and shelter for birds and beavers, and the roots of the trees helped to stabilize riverbanks. Scientists still debate how large these effects have been, but the story inspired conservationists around the world.

Rewilding is an approach to conservation that aims to restore natural processes and allow ecosystems to manage themselves. Instead of carefully controlling nature, rewilding projects often reintroduce missing species, remove barriers such as dams, and give land back to natural forces.

In Europe, rewilding projects have brought back animals such as bison, wild horses, and beavers to areas where they had disappeared centuries ago. Beavers are particularly valued, because their dams create wetlands that support many other species and can help reduce flooding downstream.

However, rewilding is not without controversy. Farmers sometimes worry that returning predators, such as wolves and bears, will attack their animals. Some people fear that rewilding means abandoning land that has been farmed for generations. Others question whether it is possible to recreate ecosystems that existed hundreds of years ago in a world that has changed so much.

Supporters argue that rewilding can work alongside farming and local communities, bringing new opportunities through tourism. At a time when many species are in decline, they believe it offers a hopeful vision: that with a little help, nature can recover.

=== b2-the-art-of-negotiation | How to Negotiate Successfully | Business | B2
Whether we are buying a car, asking for a raise, or deciding with a partner where to go on vacation, we all negotiate regularly. Yet many people feel uncomfortable negotiating and avoid it whenever possible. Research suggests that by learning a few key principles, almost anyone can become a more effective negotiator.

The first principle is preparation. Before any negotiation, it is essential to know what you want and what you are willing to accept. Negotiation experts often talk about your "best alternative," meaning what you will do if no agreement is reached. If you know you have a good alternative, such as another job offer, you can negotiate with much more confidence.

Second, it is important to understand the other side. What do they want, and why? Often, the positions that people take hide deeper interests. In a famous example, two people argue over an orange, and they finally cut it in half. Later, they discover that one wanted the juice and the other wanted the peel to bake a cake. If they had talked about their interests rather than their positions, both could have had everything they needed.

Third, the first offer matters. Studies show that the first number mentioned in a negotiation tends to act as an anchor that influences the rest of the discussion. For this reason, it is often an advantage to make the first offer, as long as you have done your research and the offer is ambitious but realistic.

Fourth, listen more than you talk. Asking open questions and paying careful attention to the answers can reveal valuable information and show the other person that you respect them. Silence can also be a powerful tool. Many people feel uncomfortable with silence and will offer more information or a better deal to fill it.

Finally, remember that negotiation does not have to be a battle. The most successful negotiators often focus on finding solutions that benefit both sides. This is especially important when you will continue to work with the other person in the future. A negotiation in which one side feels cheated may win a short-term victory, but damage a long-term relationship.

=== b2-the-dark-side-of-tourism | When Tourists Become a Problem | Travel | B2
For many cities and regions, tourism is a vital source of income. It creates jobs, supports local businesses, and can help to preserve historic buildings and traditions. But in recent years, some of the world's most popular destinations have begun to struggle with a new problem: too many visitors. The phenomenon has been given a name: overtourism.

In cities such as Venice, Barcelona, and Amsterdam, residents complain that their neighborhoods have been transformed. Narrow streets are crowded with tour groups, traditional shops have been replaced by souvenir stores, and the noise of tourists can continue late into the night. Short-term rental apartments have pushed up housing prices, forcing many local people to move out of city centers. In Venice, the population of the historic center has fallen sharply over the past few decades, while the number of tourists has grown enormously.

Natural sites are also under pressure. Beaches, mountains, and national parks can be damaged by large numbers of visitors. In Thailand, a famous bay that appeared in a Hollywood film had to be closed for several years to allow its coral reefs and marine life to recover. On some popular mountain trails, long lines of hikers have caused erosion and pollution.

Several destinations have introduced measures to manage the problem. Venice now charges day visitors an entrance fee on busy days, and large cruise ships have been banned from entering the city's center. Amsterdam has restricted holiday rentals and stopped allowing new souvenir shops in the historic center. Some places limit the number of daily visitors or require them to book in advance.

Travelers can also make a difference. Experts suggest visiting popular destinations outside the peak season, staying longer in one place rather than making short trips to many, and exploring lesser-known towns and regions. Choosing locally owned accommodation and restaurants helps to ensure that tourism money benefits local communities.

Tourism can be a powerful force for good, broadening our understanding of the world and supporting economies. But as more people than ever are able to travel, finding a balance between the needs of visitors and residents has become one of the great challenges of our time.
