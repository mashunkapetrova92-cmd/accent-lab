=== b1-the-woman-who-swam-the-channel | Swimming Against the Odds | History | B1
In nineteen twenty-six, a nineteen-year-old American called Gertrude Ederle walked into the cold, gray water on the coast of France. Her goal was to swim across the English Channel to England, a distance of about thirty-four kilometers. At that time, only five people had ever done it, and all of them were men.

Many people believed that women were not strong enough for such a challenge. One newspaper even wrote that women should accept that they would always be the weaker sex in sport. Gertrude had already won Olympic medals in swimming, but this was something different. The water was freezing, the currents were strong, and there were jellyfish. The year before, her first attempt had failed when her coach pulled her out of the water.

This time, she was determined. She covered her body in grease to protect herself from the cold and wore special goggles that she had designed herself. As she swam, the weather got worse, and strong winds made the waves very rough. Her team on a nearby boat begged her to give up. "What for?" she shouted back, and kept swimming.

After fourteen hours and thirty-nine minutes, she walked out of the sea onto an English beach. Not only was she the first woman to swim the Channel, she had beaten the men's record by almost two hours.

When she returned to New York, around two million people came out to welcome her with a huge parade. Gertrude's swim changed many people's ideas about what women could achieve. Her record lasted for more than twenty years.

=== b1-what-your-handwriting-says | The Science of First Impressions | Psychology | B1
They say you should never judge a book by its cover. But when we meet new people, most of us do exactly that. Research shows that we form a first impression of another person in a very short time, sometimes in less than a tenth of a second.

In that short moment, our brain makes quick judgments about whether a person is friendly, trustworthy, or confident. These judgments are based on many small details: their facial expression, their clothes, the way they stand, and the sound of their voice. We are usually not aware that we are doing this. It happens automatically.

This ability probably developed because it helped our ancestors survive. Thousands of years ago, people needed to decide very quickly whether a stranger was a friend or an enemy. Today, we use the same system in very different situations, such as job interviews, business meetings, and first dates.

The problem is that first impressions are often wrong. We may judge someone as unfriendly simply because they are nervous, or trust someone because they are attractive. Our first impressions can also be influenced by stereotypes and prejudices. And once we have formed an opinion, we tend to notice things that support it and ignore things that don't.

So what can we do? Psychologists suggest giving people a second chance and trying to get to know them better before making a final judgment. And if you want to make a good first impression yourself, the advice is simple: smile, make eye contact, and show genuine interest in the other person. Most people respond warmly to someone who seems interested in them.

=== b1-the-floating-market | The Floating Markets of Thailand | Travel | B1
In many countries, people do their shopping in supermarkets or street markets. But in parts of Thailand, some markets take place on the water. At floating markets, sellers paddle small wooden boats along the canals, offering fruit, vegetables, flowers, and freshly cooked food to customers on the banks or in other boats.

For hundreds of years, canals were the main roads in central Thailand. Many people lived in houses built over the water, and boats were the easiest way to travel and to trade. Farmers brought their products to markets by boat, and families did their shopping without ever stepping on land. As roads and cars became more common in the twentieth century, many of these markets disappeared.

Today, some floating markets have become popular tourist attractions. The most famous ones, near Bangkok, are often crowded with visitors taking photos and buying souvenirs. Some travelers feel that these markets have lost their traditional character. But there are still smaller markets where local people do their everyday shopping.

Visiting a floating market early in the morning is an unforgettable experience. Women in wide straw hats sell piles of mangoes, bananas, and coconuts from their boats. Others cook noodle soup on small stoves right on the water and pass the bowls up to customers with long poles. The air is full of the smell of grilled meat and sweet coconut.

If you visit, try to go early, before the tour buses arrive. And don't forget to try the mango with sticky rice, a delicious local dessert that tastes even better when you eat it beside the water.

=== b1-the-surprising-benefits-of-cold | Is Cold Water Good for You? | Health | B1
Every winter, in many countries, groups of people gather at lakes, rivers, and beaches and jump into the freezing water. To most people, this sounds like a terrible idea. But cold water swimming has become increasingly popular, and many people say it has changed their lives.

When you get into very cold water, your body reacts immediately. Your heart rate increases, and you start breathing very fast. This is called cold shock, and it can be dangerous, especially for people with heart problems. That is why experts say you should never jump straight in, but enter the water slowly and stay for only a short time at first.

So why do people do it? Many swimmers say that cold water makes them feel energetic and happy for hours afterward. Scientists think this may be because cold water causes the body to release chemicals that improve our mood. Some people believe that regular cold swimming helps them cope with stress and feel less anxious. There is also some evidence that it might strengthen the immune system, although more research is needed.

For many swimmers, the social side is just as important. Cold water swimming groups are often very friendly communities. Members encourage each other, share hot drinks after their swim, and become close friends.

If you want to try it, it is important to be careful. Never swim alone, choose a safe place, and get out of the water before you start shivering badly. Having warm clothes ready is essential. Some people prefer to start more gently by ending their normal shower with thirty seconds of cold water.

=== b1-the-one-hundred-year-old-runner | Never Too Old | Health | B1
Most people think of running marathons as something for young, fit athletes. But in two thousand eleven, a man called Fauja Singh changed many people's ideas about age when he finished a marathon in Toronto at the age of one hundred.

Fauja was born in a village in India. As a child, he was weak and couldn't walk properly until he was five years old. He spent most of his life as a farmer. He had never run a race. Then, in his eighties, his life changed completely. His wife and his son died, and he was heartbroken. He moved to London to live with another son and began to feel lonely and sad.

To deal with his sadness, he started running. At first, he ran short distances, but he soon discovered that he loved it. At the age of eighty-nine, he ran his first marathon in London. He finished it in a little over six and a half hours, and he went on to run several more.

When people asked him about his secret, Fauja always gave simple answers. He ate a light vegetarian diet, drank plenty of water and tea, and went to bed early. He also said that being happy and avoiding negative people was very important. "I don't think about the past," he once said. "I think about the next race."

Fauja's achievements made him famous around the world. He carried the Olympic torch in London and appeared in advertisements for a sports company. More importantly, he became an inspiration to older people everywhere, showing that it is never too late to start something new.

=== b1-the-perfect-cup-of-tea | The Story of Tea | Culture | B1
After water, tea is the most popular drink in the world. Every day, people drink billions of cups of it. But the history of tea goes back thousands of years, and it has had a surprising influence on world events.

According to an old Chinese legend, tea was discovered around five thousand years ago by an emperor called Shen Nong. He was boiling water under a tree when some leaves fell into his pot. He tasted the water and found it delicious and refreshing. Whether or not this story is true, we know that people in China were drinking tea at least two thousand years ago. At first, it was used mainly as a medicine.

Tea spread from China to Japan, where Buddhist monks drank it to stay awake during long periods of meditation. Over time, the Japanese developed a special tea ceremony, a slow and careful way of preparing and serving green tea that is still practiced today.

Tea arrived in Europe in the seventeenth century, and it quickly became very fashionable, especially in Britain. At first, it was very expensive, and only rich people could afford it. To meet the growing demand, the British began growing tea in India and Sri Lanka, which are now among the largest tea producers in the world.

Tea has even played a role in politics. In seventeen seventy-three, American colonists who were angry about British taxes threw hundreds of boxes of tea into the harbor in Boston. This event, known as the Boston Tea Party, was one of the events that led to the American Revolution.

=== b1-a-phone-free-school | The School That Banned Phones | Education | B1
Two years ago, the high school in our town made a big decision. Students would no longer be allowed to use their phones during the school day. Every morning, they had to put their phones in special locked bags, which they could only open when they left school in the afternoon.

At first, many students were angry. Some said it was unfair and that they needed their phones in case of an emergency. Parents were also worried at the beginning. But the school explained that students could always use the phone in the office, and that parents could call the school if there was a problem.

After a few weeks, teachers noticed some big changes. Students concentrated better in class, and they were less distracted. During breaks, instead of looking at their screens, they talked to each other, played games, and went outside. The library became busy again. Some teachers said the atmosphere in the school felt calmer and friendlier.

There were other benefits too. Cases of online bullying during school hours fell, because students couldn't send messages or take photos of each other. Some students said they felt less pressure, because they didn't have to keep checking social media all day. By the end of the first year, exam results had also improved slightly.

Not everybody is convinced. Some people argue that young people need to learn to use technology responsibly, and that banning phones doesn't teach them how to do that. But in our town, most students now say they don't mind the rule. As one fifteen-year-old put it, "It's actually a relief to have a break."

=== b1-the-detective-novel | The Missing Painting | Short Fiction | B1
The painting had been stolen sometime between midnight and six in the morning. When the museum guard arrived for his morning shift, he found an empty space on the wall where a small but very valuable landscape had hung for fifty years. There was no sign of a break-in. The alarm had not gone off.

Detective Sarah Moss walked slowly around the room. The window was locked from the inside. The door had been opened with a key. Only four people had keys to the museum: the director, the two night guards, and the cleaner. Sarah decided to interview all of them.

The director was shocked and upset. He had been at a dinner party all evening, and many people had seen him there. The first night guard, a young man called Ryan, said he had checked the room at two o'clock, and the painting had been there. The cleaner said she had finished work at ten and gone straight home. The second guard, an older man called Peter, had been sick and had not come to work that night.

Sarah thought for a long time. Then she asked Ryan a simple question. "You said you checked the room at two o'clock. How did you know the painting was there?" Ryan looked confused. "I saw it," he said. "I turned on the light." Sarah smiled. "That's strange," she said, "because the lights in that room have been broken for a week. The director told me."

Ryan's face went white. Later that day, the police found the painting hidden in his apartment. He had planned to sell it to a collector abroad.

=== b1-the-floating-city | Venice and the Rising Sea | Environment | B1
Venice is one of the most beautiful cities in the world. Built on more than a hundred small islands in a lagoon in northern Italy, it has canals instead of streets and boats instead of cars. Every year, millions of tourists come to admire its palaces, churches, and bridges. But Venice is in serious danger.

The city has always flooded from time to time, especially in autumn and winter. When strong winds and high tides combine, seawater rises and pours into the streets and squares. Locals call this "acqua alta," which means high water. In the past, this happened occasionally. Today, it happens much more often, and the floods are getting worse.

There are two main reasons. First, the city is slowly sinking. For many years, factories pumped water from under the ground, which made the land sink faster. Second, sea levels are rising because of climate change. Together, these changes mean that the water is getting closer and closer to the city.

To protect Venice, Italy has built a huge system of barriers at the entrances to the lagoon. When a dangerous tide is expected, the barriers rise from the seabed and block the water. The project took many years and cost billions of euros, and it has already prevented several floods.

But the barriers cannot solve every problem. If sea levels continue to rise, they will need to be closed more often, which could harm the lagoon's environment. Venice also faces other challenges, such as huge numbers of tourists and a falling population. Many people are asking how the city can survive in the future.

=== b1-the-joy-of-missing-out | The Joy of Missing Out | Modern Life | B1
You have probably heard of FOMO, the "fear of missing out." It is the anxious feeling that other people are having more fun or more interesting experiences than you. Social media makes this feeling very common. When we see photos of friends at parties, on vacations, or at concerts, we can easily feel that our own lives are boring.

In recent years, some people have started talking about the opposite: JOMO, the "joy of missing out." Instead of trying to do everything and be everywhere, people who practice JOMO choose to slow down and enjoy the present moment. They say no to some invitations without feeling guilty. They spend an evening at home reading, cooking, or simply resting, and they enjoy it.

The idea is simple, but it can be surprisingly difficult. Many of us feel pressure to stay busy all the time. We worry that if we say no to an event, people will forget about us, or we will miss something important. But psychologists say that constantly trying to keep up can lead to stress, tiredness, and dissatisfaction.

Practicing JOMO doesn't mean avoiding people or never going out. It means making conscious choices about how to spend your time and energy. It means focusing on what really matters to you, instead of what you think you should be doing.

Some simple ways to start include turning off notifications on your phone, spending less time on social media, and planning some free evenings with nothing to do. You may discover that missing out is not something to be afraid of. Sometimes, it is exactly what you need.

=== b1-the-real-dracula | The Real Dracula | History | B1
Count Dracula, the vampire who drinks human blood, is one of the most famous characters in literature. He first appeared in a novel by the Irish writer Bram Stoker in eighteen ninety-seven. But many people don't know that the name Dracula comes from a real historical person, a prince who lived in the fifteenth century.

His name was Vlad the Third, and he ruled a region called Wallachia, which is now part of Romania. His father was known as Vlad Dracul, a name that comes from the word for "dragon." So Vlad the Third became known as Dracula, which means "son of the dragon."

Vlad lived in a violent time, when his small country was often at war with the powerful Ottoman Empire. He was famous for his cruel methods of punishing enemies and criminals. His most terrible habit was killing his enemies by putting them on tall wooden stakes, which gave him the nickname "Vlad the Impaler." Stories about his cruelty spread across Europe.

However, in Romania, Vlad is not only remembered as a monster. Some people see him as a national hero who defended his country against invaders and fought against corruption.

Historians are not sure how much Bram Stoker actually knew about Vlad. He probably borrowed the name after reading about it in a book, but his vampire character has little in common with the real prince. Today, thousands of tourists visit Bran Castle in Romania, which is often called "Dracula's Castle." Ironically, there is no strong evidence that Vlad ever lived there.

=== b1-learning-a-language-as-an-adult | Is It Too Late to Learn a Language? | Education | B1
Many adults would like to learn a new language, but they believe it is too late. They have heard that children learn languages easily, while adults find it much harder. Is this true? The answer, according to researchers, is more complicated than most people think.

It is true that young children have some advantages. If they grow up hearing a language, they usually learn to speak it without a foreign accent. Their brains are very good at recognizing new sounds, and they are not afraid of making mistakes. They also have a lot of time to practice, often hours every day.

But adults have their own advantages. They already know how language works, so they can understand grammar rules and use them more quickly. They can use dictionaries, apps, and books. They also have more experience of learning in general, and they can plan their studies and stay motivated. Some studies show that adults actually learn faster than children in the early stages of learning a language.

The biggest difference is often not the brain, but the situation. Children are usually surrounded by the language, while adults are busy with work and family and have less time to practice. Adults are also more likely to feel embarrassed when they make mistakes, which can stop them from speaking.

So what is the best advice for adult learners? Practice a little every day, rather than a lot once a week. Listen to the language as much as possible, through music, podcasts, and films. And most importantly, don't be afraid to make mistakes. Every mistake is a step toward speaking better.

=== b1-the-kindest-town | The Town That Adopted Strangers | Society | B1
On the morning of September eleventh, two thousand one, about forty airplanes were flying over the Atlantic Ocean toward the United States when the American government closed its airspace. The planes were told to land immediately at the nearest airport. Many of them were sent to a small town in Canada called Gander.

Gander had a population of about nine thousand people. Suddenly, around seven thousand passengers and crew from all over the world arrived at its small airport. They were tired, frightened, and confused, and many didn't know what had happened. They would have to stay in Gander for several days.

The people of Gander responded with incredible generosity. Schools, churches, and community centers were turned into temporary shelters. Families opened their homes to strangers, offering them beds, showers, and meals. Local bakeries and restaurants cooked huge amounts of food for free. Pharmacists provided medicine, and residents brought clothes, blankets, and toys for the children. Some passengers were even taken on trips to see the local area.

Many passengers later said that the kindness they experienced in Gander was something they would never forget. Strong friendships formed between the passengers and the local people, and some of them still visit each other today. Several passengers created scholarships for students in the town to say thank you.

The story of Gander became so well known that it was turned into a successful musical called "Come From Away," which has been performed around the world. It reminds audiences that even on one of the darkest days in recent history, ordinary people chose kindness.

=== b1-the-first-photograph | The Invention of Photography | Technology | B1
Today, most of us carry a camera in our pocket and take dozens of photos every day. But taking a picture was not always so easy. The very first photograph that still exists took around eight hours to make.

That photograph was taken in eighteen twenty-six or eighteen twenty-seven by a French inventor called Joseph Nicephore Niepce. He placed a metal plate covered in a special chemical inside a box with a small hole, and pointed it out of a window at his house. The light slowly changed the chemical on the plate, creating a blurry image of the buildings and trees outside. Because it took so long, the sun moved across the sky, and both sides of the buildings appear to be lit.

A few years later, another Frenchman, Louis Daguerre, developed a much faster method. His photographs, called daguerreotypes, took only a few minutes and were very sharp. They became extremely popular, and people lined up in studios to have their portraits taken. However, they still had to sit completely still for a long time, which is why people in old photographs rarely smile.

Over the following decades, photography became faster, cheaper, and easier. In eighteen eighty-eight, an American company introduced a small, simple camera that anybody could use, with the slogan "You press the button, we do the rest." Photography was no longer just for professionals.

The arrival of digital cameras and then smartphones changed everything again. Today, people take more photos every few minutes than were taken in the entire nineteenth century.

=== b1-the-sound-of-silence | In Search of Silence | Modern Life | B1
When was the last time you experienced complete silence? For most people who live in cities, it is almost impossible to remember. Traffic, construction work, music, voices, and the sound of our phones surround us almost all the time. Some scientists believe that this constant noise is having a negative effect on our health.

Research has shown that long-term exposure to noise, especially traffic noise, can increase stress, disturb our sleep, and even raise the risk of heart disease. Children who go to schools near airports or busy roads sometimes have more difficulty concentrating and learning to read. Noise pollution is now considered one of the biggest environmental health problems in Europe.

On the other hand, silence seems to be good for us. In one study, scientists found that two minutes of silence was more relaxing for the body than listening to calm music. Other studies suggest that silence may help the brain to process information and think more clearly.

Because of this, some people are now actively looking for quiet places. Some travel to remote areas in nature, such as deserts or mountains, where they can escape all human sounds. In some cities, libraries and parks have created special quiet zones. A few organizations even certify "quiet parks" where natural sounds are protected.

You don't need to travel far to find a little more silence in your life, though. You could turn off the television when you are not watching it, take a walk without headphones, or spend a few minutes each morning sitting quietly before you check your phone. Even small moments of quiet can help you feel calmer.

=== b1-the-dog-who-waited | The Dog Who Waited | Animals | B1
In nineteen twenty-four, a professor at the University of Tokyo called Hidesaburo Ueno adopted a small white puppy. He named the dog Hachiko. The two became very close. Every morning, Hachiko walked with the professor to Shibuya train station, and every afternoon, the dog waited at the station for him to come home from work.

One day in May nineteen twenty-five, the professor did not come home. He had suffered a sudden illness at work and died. Hachiko waited at the station that evening, but his owner never appeared. The next day, the dog went back to the station at the same time and waited again.

Hachiko continued to do this every day for nearly ten years. At first, some station workers were not kind to him. But in nineteen thirty-two, a newspaper published a story about the loyal dog, and he became famous across Japan. People started bringing him food, and visitors came from far away to see him. Teachers and parents told children about Hachiko as an example of loyalty.

Hachiko died in nineteen thirty-five, still waiting near the station. A bronze statue of him had been placed there a year earlier, and he was present at the ceremony. Today, the statue is one of the most famous meeting places in Tokyo, and thousands of people walk past it every day.

Hachiko's story has inspired books and films in many countries. In two thousand fifteen, the University of Tokyo created a new statue showing Hachiko finally being reunited with his owner, jumping up happily to greet him.

=== b1-how-to-be-a-better-listener | How to Be a Good Listener | Psychology | B1
Most of us think we are good listeners. But in reality, many people don't really listen when others are talking. Instead, they are thinking about what they want to say next, checking their phone, or waiting for a chance to tell their own story. Good listening is a skill that can improve our relationships at home and at work.

The first step is to give the other person your full attention. Put your phone away and look at them. This simple action shows that you respect them and care about what they are saying. Try not to interrupt, even if you think you know what they are going to say. Let them finish their thoughts.

Good listeners also show that they are listening. You can nod, smile, or make small sounds like "mm" or "I see." Asking questions is very helpful too, especially open questions like "How did you feel about that?" or "What happened next?" These questions encourage people to say more and show that you are interested.

Another useful technique is to repeat back what you have heard in your own words. For example, you might say, "So you're saying that you felt ignored by your boss?" This helps you check that you have understood correctly, and it makes the other person feel understood.

Finally, resist the urge to give advice immediately or to turn the conversation toward yourself. Often, people don't want solutions. They simply want somebody to listen. As the saying goes, we have two ears and one mouth, so perhaps we should listen twice as much as we speak.

=== b1-the-mountain-rescue | Rescue on the Mountain | Short Fiction | B1
Anna and her brother Mark had been planning their hike for months. The trail was long and challenging, but they were both experienced walkers. They set off early in the morning under a clear blue sky, carrying plenty of water, food, and warm clothes.

By the early afternoon, they had reached the top. The view was breathtaking. They could see lakes and valleys stretching for kilometers in every direction. They ate their lunch on a rock and took photos. But as they began to walk down, the weather suddenly changed. Dark clouds appeared, and within minutes, thick fog covered the mountain. They could hardly see the path in front of them.

Then Mark slipped on a wet rock and fell. When he tried to stand, he cried out in pain. His ankle was badly injured, and he couldn't walk. Anna tried to call for help, but there was no signal. She knew they couldn't stay where they were for long, because the temperature was dropping fast.

Anna made a difficult decision. She wrapped Mark in all their warm clothes and a survival blanket, gave him most of the water, and marked the spot with her bright red jacket. Then she walked carefully down the mountain until she found a signal and called the mountain rescue team.

Two hours later, a rescue team found Mark, cold but safe. They carried him down on a stretcher. At the hospital, the doctor told them that Anna's quick thinking had probably saved his life. Mark's ankle healed, and the following summer, they climbed the mountain again, this time checking the weather forecast twice.

=== b1-the-world-of-emojis | The Rise of Emojis | Technology | B1
Today, billions of people use emojis every day. These small pictures of faces, animals, food, and objects have become a normal part of the way we communicate, especially in text messages and on social media. But where did emojis come from, and are they changing the way we use language?

The first set of emojis was created in Japan in the late nineteen nineties by a designer called Shigetaka Kurita. He was working for a mobile phone company and wanted to create a simple way for people to express emotions in short messages. His first set contained one hundred and seventy-six simple images, including a smiling face, a heart, and the sun. Today, some of them are kept in the collection of a famous museum in New York.

Emojis became popular around the world when smartphones started to include them in the early two thousand tens. Now there are thousands of different emojis, and new ones are added every year. Anybody can suggest a new emoji, but it has to be approved by an organization that sets international standards for computer text.

Emojis are useful because written messages can easily be misunderstood. Without seeing someone's face or hearing their voice, it can be hard to tell if they are joking, angry, or serious. A simple smiling face can make a message feel friendlier.

However, emojis can also cause confusion. Some emojis look different on different phones, and people from different cultures may understand them in different ways. A thumbs up, for example, is positive in many countries but can be rude in others. Like any language, emojis need to be used with care.

=== b1-a-farm-in-the-city | A Farm in the Middle of the City | Environment | B1
When we think of farms, we usually imagine green fields, tractors, and cows in the countryside. But in cities around the world, a new kind of farming is growing quickly. Urban farms are appearing on rooftops, in empty buildings, and even underground.

One of the most interesting types is vertical farming. Instead of growing plants in fields, vertical farms grow them indoors, in tall stacks of shelves. The plants grow under special LED lights that replace sunlight, and their roots sit in water full of nutrients rather than in soil. Computers carefully control the temperature, light, and water.

Vertical farms have several advantages. They can produce a lot of food in a very small space, and they can grow crops all year round, whatever the weather outside. Because they are indoors, they don't need pesticides, and they use much less water than traditional farms. And since they are located in cities, the food doesn't have to travel long distances to reach the shops, so it is fresher.

However, vertical farms also have problems. The lights and computers use a lot of electricity, which is expensive and can be bad for the environment unless the energy comes from renewable sources. At the moment, vertical farms are mainly used to grow salad leaves and herbs, which grow quickly. It is much more difficult to grow foods like wheat or potatoes in this way.

Other urban farms are simpler. In many cities, community groups grow vegetables on empty land or keep bees on rooftops. As the world's cities continue to grow, these projects may become an important part of feeding their populations.
